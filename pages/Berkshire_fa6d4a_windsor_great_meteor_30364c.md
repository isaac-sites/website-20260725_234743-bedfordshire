## Introduction

The brilliant object seen from Windsor Castle on 18 August 1783 was not a convincing early encounter with an alien craft. It was one view of an exceptionally bright meteor observed across Britain and parts of continental Europe. From Windsor’s North Terrace, Tiberius Cavallo and his companions saw a luminous body brighten, cross the sky, illuminate the landscape and divide into smaller glowing masses. Reports gathered from other locations described the same short-lived phenomenon from different angles. Royal Society investigators then compared directions, timings and apparent elevations to reconstruct a long atmospheric path.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://royalsocietypublishing.org/rstl/article/doi/10.1098/rstl.1784.0010/120712/IX-Description-of-a-meteor-observed-Aug" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: royalsocietypublishing.org">[royalsocietypublishing.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">royalsocietypublishing.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Royal Society Publishing IX</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Description of a meteor, observed Aug. 18, 1783 &#124; Philosophical Transactions &#124; The Royal SocietyIX. The case matters to [Berkshire]({{ 'what-really-happened-in-berkshires-ufo/' | relative_url }})’s UFO history because modern retellings often isolate the most unusual phrases in the Windsor account—particularly an apparent pause and change of direction—while overlooking the wider evidence. Once the observations are reunited, the supposed Windsor UFO becomes a valuable historical example of how a spectacular unidentified sighting can be explained through comparison rather than speculation.

## What witnesses saw from Windsor Castle

At about 9.17 pm, Cavallo was standing at the north-east corner of the terrace at Windsor Castle with physician James Lind, Dr Lockman, artist Thomas Sandby and several other people. The weather was calm and the sky largely clear, although haze lay close to the horizon. Cavallo later wrote that the group had an unobstructed view and that their combined recollections formed the basis of his report.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://royalsocietypublishing.org/rstl/article/doi/10.1098/rstl.1784.0010/120712/IX-Description-of-a-meteor-observed-Aug" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: royalsocietypublishing.org">[Royal Society Publishing]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">royalsocietypublishing.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Royal Society Publishing IX</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Description of a meteor, observed Aug. 18, 1783 &#124; Philosophical Transactions &#124; The Royal SocietyIX. The first visible feature was not a metallic machine or a sharply outlined vessel. Cavallo described faint, shifting light in the northern sky, rather like an auroral glow, associated with a round luminous body near the horizon. Its apparent diameter was roughly half that of the Moon. It initially seemed almost stationary, then grew brighter and began moving through the sky. After a short oblique rise, it followed a longer course generally eastwards before turning towards the south-east and disappearing. The entire display lasted less than half a minute.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://royalsocietypublishing.org/rstl/article/doi/10.1098/rstl.1784.0010/120712/IX-Description-of-a-meteor-observed-Aug" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: royalsocietypublishing.org">[Royal Society Publishing]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">royalsocietypublishing.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Royal Society Publishing IX</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Description of a meteor, observed Aug. 18, 1783 &#124; Philosophical Transactions &#124; The Royal SocietyIX. At its brightest, the object illuminated the country visible from the terrace. Cavallo also reported that the main body was followed by several smaller luminous masses, giving the appearance of fragmentation or a procession. Some minutes later, the observers heard a distant rumbling resembling thunder, which Cavallo thought might have been connected with an explosion of the meteor.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://royalsocietypublishing.org/rstl/article/doi/10.1098/rstl.1784.0010/120712/IX-Description-of-a-meteor-observed-Aug" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: royalsocietypublishing.org">[Royal Society Publishing]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">royalsocietypublishing.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Royal Society Publishing IX</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Description of a meteor, observed Aug. 18, 1783 &#124; Philosophical Transactions &#124; The Royal SocietyIX. Those details explain why the story remains memorable. The phenomenon was bright enough to alter the appearance of the landscape, complex enough to change shape, and slow enough for several witnesses to follow its progress. Nevertheless, none of those characteristics is incompatible with an unusually large fireball travelling a long, shallow path through the upper atmosphere.

The language of the original account also needs historical care. In eighteenth-century scientific writing, *meteor* was a broad term for a phenomenon occurring in the atmosphere, not necessarily a small streak of light lasting a fraction of a second. Cavallo repeatedly treated the Windsor object as a meteor and submitted his observations to the Royal Society as material for scientific comparison, not as evidence of an artificial vehicle.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://makingscience.royalsociety.org/items/l-and-p_8_50/paper-a-meteor-observed-at-windsor-28-august-1783-by-tiberius-cavallo" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: makingscience.royalsociety.org">[Making Science]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">makingscience.royalsociety.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">paper a meteor observed at windsor 28 august 1783 by tiberius cavallo</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Tiberius Cavallo, Paper, &#x27;A meteor observed at Windsor 28 August 1783&#x27; by Tiberius Cavallo, 21 August 1783, L&amp;P/8/50, The Royal Society A...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: august 1783</span></span></span>

## How contemporary scientists compared reports

The decisive evidence did not come from Windsor alone. The event was observed over a very large area, producing reports from Yorkshire, Lincolnshire, London, Kent and other parts of Britain, as well as continental Europe. The Royal Society published several accounts in the same volume of *Philosophical Transactions*, including observations by Cavallo, Alexander Aubert, William Cooper, Richard Lovell Edgeworth and Nathaniel Pigott.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://royalsocietypublishing.org/rstl/issue/number/74" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: royalsocietypublishing.org">[royalsocietypublishing.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">royalsocietypublishing.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Royal Society PublishingVolume Issue 74 &#124; Philosophical Transactions &#124; The Royal Societyremarks upon the experiments on air Philosophical...</span></span></span>

This geographical spread transformed the problem. A strange light seen from one terrace might leave enormous room for interpretation. The same light seen almost simultaneously from distant places, moving along mutually consistent lines of sight, could be treated as a physical object following a calculable course.

Aubert explicitly explained the value of returning to the observation point and using landmarks and instruments to estimate the object’s direction and elevation. He also acknowledged the limitations of unexpected eyewitness testimony: no observer could be expected to measure every feature accurately while surprised by a sudden event. His approach was therefore to reconstruct the sighting afterwards from fixed reference points rather than treat an unaided first impression as exact.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://royalsocietypublishing.org/rstl/article/doi/10.1098/rstl.1784.0011/120724/X-An-account-of-the-meteors-of-the-18th-of-August" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: royalsocietypublishing.org">[Royal Society Publishing]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">royalsocietypublishing.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">X An account of the meteors of the 18th of August</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">X An account of the meteors of the 18th of August</span></span></span>

Charles Blagden went further by gathering and comparing reports received by Royal Society president Joseph Banks. The correspondence network included letters, drawings, newspaper notices and observations from Britain and abroad. From this material Blagden attempted to estimate the meteor’s height, speed, dimensions and route. His calculations were necessarily rough, but the method was recognisably scientific: identify reports likely to describe the same event, compare their viewing geometry and look for a trajectory capable of reconciling them.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/details/philtrans06546183" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[archive.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Internet Archive An Account of Some Late Fiery Meteors; With Observations</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">In a Letter from Charles Blagden, M. The resulting reconstruction placed the body high above the ground and travelling at extreme speed along an extended path. Historical summaries of Blagden’s work give an estimated altitude of roughly 50 to 60 miles and a speed near 20 miles per second. The precise figures should not be treated as modern-quality measurements, but their scale is unmistakably meteoric rather than aeronautical.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arts.st-andrews.ac.uk/philosophicaltransactions/crowd-sourcing-eighteenth-century-science-the-great-fireball-of-1783/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arts.st-andrews.ac.uk">[arts.st-andrews.ac.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arts.st-andrews.ac.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">crowd sourcing eighteenth century science the great fireball of 1783</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">That same summer a large meteor was seen over England on the night of August 18^{th}, passing rapidly over Scotland and travelling down t...</span></span></span>

Reports from different locations also help explain features that appear puzzling in the Windsor description:

* **The apparent pause:** A distant body travelling nearly towards an observer can show little sideways movement against the sky. As its path changes relative to the observer’s line of sight—or as it emerges more clearly from haze—its angular motion can suddenly become obvious.

<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **The apparent rise:** An object following a shallow atmospheric trajectory may seem to climb from one viewpoint even while descending in physical altitude. Apparent direction on the sky is not the same as three-dimensional motion.
* **The turn towards the south-east:** A long, curved-looking track can result from perspective across the celestial sphere. The reported change does not require a powered turn by a controlled craft.
* **The smaller following bodies:** Fragmentation is a familiar feature of bright meteors. Pieces and glowing debris may separate from the main mass and continue along closely related paths.
* **The delayed rumble:** Sound travels far more slowly than light. A substantial delay between the flash and a distant boom is therefore consistent with an energetic event occurring many miles away.

</div>

No single one of these points proves the meteor explanation. Together with the wide distribution of observations and the reconstructed high-speed path, however, they form a much stronger case than the isolated wording used in later UFO accounts.

## The Sandby image records a sequence, not a formation

The Windsor event also survives in unusually striking visual form. Thomas and Paul Sandby produced drawings and prints showing spectators on the castle terrace beneath three luminous forms marked at successive positions in the sky. The British Museum describes the print as representing the meteor’s progression at points labelled A, B and C. A related watercolour likewise shows the object in three stages during its passage.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/P" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: britishmuseum.org">[British Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">britishmuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on britishmuseum.org.</span></span></span>

This is important because reproductions are sometimes viewed as though they depict three objects present at the same moment, or one object hovering and changing shape above Windsor. That is not what the composition was intended to show. It is a time-sequence diagram built into a single landscape: several moments of one moving event have been placed together so that the viewer can follow its course.

The technique was practical in an age before photography. An artist could preserve the setting, the observers and successive stages of a transient phenomenon in one image. It provided more information than a conventional landscape, but it was not a frame-by-frame mechanical record. The spacing, scale and shape of the glowing forms should therefore not be measured as though the picture were a modern long-exposure photograph.

The attribution history is also more complicated than some UFO retellings suggest. Museum catalogues discuss contributions by both Sandby brothers, with Thomas associated with drawings and Paul with the published aquatint, while acknowledging uncertainty over particular surviving works. What is secure is that Thomas was among Cavallo’s companions and that the finished print was produced soon after the event, in 1783.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/P" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: britishmuseum.org">[British Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">britishmuseum.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on britishmuseum.org.</span></span></span>

The image is excellent historical evidence that the Windsor group saw a dramatic luminous passage. It is weak evidence for artificial flight because it was constructed to illustrate stages described in a meteor report. Removing it from that documentary setting reverses its original purpose.

## Why later UFO retellings changed the story

The Windsor episode entered UFO culture through a familiar process: an old account containing unusual motion was detached from the wider investigation and rewritten in modern technological language. Some websites call the object a sphere that emerged from a cloud, halted, changed direction and flew away. Others emphasise that trained or distinguished observers were present, implying that their status rules out a meteor.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.beamsinvestigations.org/Windsor%20Castle%20UFO%20or%20Meteor%201783.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: beamsinvestigations.org">[beamsinvestigations.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">beamsinvestigations.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Windsor Castle UFO or Meteor 1783</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Windsor Castle UFO or Meteor 1783</span></span></span>

Several changes make these versions sound more mysterious than the primary record.

**“Almost stationary” becomes “stopped.”** Cavallo’s wording concerns apparent motion near the horizon. It does not establish that the body physically halted in the atmosphere. A near-stationary appearance is particularly vulnerable to perspective effects when the observer does not know the object’s distance or direction of travel.

**A luminous body becomes a solid craft.** The contemporary account describes light, changing brightness, a trail-like display and fragmentation. It does not report windows, a manufactured surface, occupants, engine noise or a clearly bounded structure.

**A short visual sequence becomes purposeful manoeuvring.** The reported changes occurred during an event lasting under 30 seconds. Later summaries often break the passage into stages—arrival, hover, turn and departure—that resemble controlled actions. That interpretation is imposed by the retelling rather than demonstrated by the observation.

**The Windsor witnesses become the only witnesses.** In reality, the broad sighting distribution is the strongest evidence in the case. Treating Windsor as an isolated encounter suppresses the very information that allowed eighteenth-century investigators to identify a common atmospheric event.

**Scientific uncertainty becomes scientific disagreement.** Cavallo, Aubert and Blagden did not know everything about meteors. Their estimates varied and some reports were inconsistent. But incomplete knowledge is not evidence that they were describing an engineered object. Their uncertainty concerned the meteor’s exact height, size, path and physical nature—not whether it behaved like a visiting spacecraft.

One modern retelling even argues that Royal Society Fellows should have been able to recognise an ordinary meteor. That misses the historical point. The display was not ordinary, and meteor science was still developing. The observers recognised it as an extraordinary atmospheric phenomenon and responded by collecting data. Their willingness to investigate an unfamiliar meteor should not be recast as an inability to identify an obvious machine.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.stronghold-nation.com/history/myth/the-1783-england-mass-sighting" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: stronghold-nation.com">[Stronghold Nation]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">stronghold-nation.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">the 1783 england mass sighting</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">the 1783 england mass sighting</span></span></span>

## Did the object really change direction?

The claimed change of direction is the most important surviving argument for a UFO interpretation. Read alone, Cavallo’s description of an initial oblique rise followed by eastward and then south-easterly motion may sound like a sequence of deliberate turns. Yet eyewitness directions are apparent positions projected on to the sky, not a map of the object’s physical course.

A long meteor moving through three-dimensional space can produce a curved or changing angular track for several reasons. The observer is looking from a fixed point at a distant body whose range and bearing are changing rapidly. The horizon and local landscape provide reference lines, but they do not reveal altitude or distance. Haze may conceal the earliest portion of the track, while increasing brightness makes later movement easier to see. Fragmentation can also shift attention from the original body to a brighter piece travelling along a slightly different-looking line.

Most importantly, the reports from other places did not reveal an object circling Windsor or reversing course over Berkshire. They were consistent enough for investigators to infer a broad, rapid passage across a substantial part of Europe. The object’s movement therefore cannot be judged solely from the shape traced against Windsor’s horizon.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://archive.org/details/philtrans06546183" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archive.org">[Internet Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Internet Archive An Account of Some Late Fiery Meteors; With Observations</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">In a Letter from Charles Blagden, M. There were occasional contradictory reports. One later account attributed to a naval observer north of Ireland even suggested that the meteor was subsequently seen moving back in the opposite direction. Contemporary compilers recognised that some testimony was inconsistent and declined to treat every detail as equally dependable. That selectivity is a strength, not a cover-up: mass sightings regularly contain timing errors, direction errors and accidental combinations of separate phenomena.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1783_Great_Meteor" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The meteor was the subject of much discussion i</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">1783 Great MeteorThe 1783 Great Meteor was a meteor procession observed on 18 August 1783 from the British Isles, at a time when such phe...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 1783</span></span></span>

For the UFO claim to outweigh the meteor explanation, the apparent turn would have to survive comparison with the complete observation set and require manoeuvring that no plausible meteoric path could reproduce. The surviving evidence does not reach that standard.

## Why the Windsor case matters in Berkshire

Within historic Berkshire, the 1783 event is significant not because it remains an unresolved UFO case, but because it offers an unusually complete demonstration of identification in practice. Windsor supplied vivid eyewitness testimony and a celebrated image. Other locations supplied independent viewing angles. The Royal Society provided a network for gathering reports, and Blagden attempted a geographical reconstruction.

The Windsor meteor also shows why witness credibility and witness interpretation must be kept separate. Cavallo, Lind and Sandby were serious and capable observers. Their credibility supports the conclusion that an extraordinary luminous event occurred. It does not make every estimate of distance, shape or motion exact, nor does it support later claims that they saw technology. Reliable people can accurately report an experience while understandably misjudging aspects of a distant object seen without preparation.

In that sense, the case is more useful to Berkshire’s UFO history after the alien interpretation is removed. It provides a documented control case: a spectacle that could easily sound anomalous when reduced to one witness account, but becomes recognisable when reports from a much wider area are assembled.

## The most reasonable verdict

Windsor’s famous “UFO” was almost certainly the Great Meteor of 18 August 1783. The conclusion rests on converging evidence rather than a convenient label: a brief luminous passage, intense ground illumination, fragmentation, possible delayed sound, independent reports over a large geographical area and a contemporary reconstruction indicating a high-altitude object moving at meteoric speed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://royalsocietypublishing.org/rstl/article/doi/10.1098/rstl.1784.0010/120712/IX-Description-of-a-meteor-observed-Aug" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: royalsocietypublishing.org">[royalsocietypublishing.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">royalsocietypublishing.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Royal Society Publishing IX</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Description of a meteor, observed Aug. 18, 1783 &#124; Philosophical Transactions &#124; The Royal SocietyIX. Some details remain uncertain. Eighteenth-century timings were not perfectly synchronised, estimates of altitude and size were crude, and eyewitness accounts disagreed over colours, directions and duration. The Sandby pictures are interpretations of a sequence rather than instrument readings. Those limitations prevent a precise modern reconstruction, but they do not create a balanced contest between a meteor and an artificial craft.

Later UFO versions weakened the case by narrowing it. They elevated a few ambiguous phrases, treated a sequential artwork as literal aerial geometry and left out the observations that connected Windsor to a much larger fireball event. The best surviving records therefore turn the supposed mystery on its head: Windsor was not the site of an inexplicable vehicle whose true nature was later concealed, but one observation point in an early and notably successful attempt to explain a spectacular object seen across the sky.
