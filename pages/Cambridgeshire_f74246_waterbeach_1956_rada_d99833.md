## Introduction

RAF Waterbeach’s role in the famous East Anglian radar mystery of 13–14 August 1956 was real, but it was not as straightforward as later UFO accounts often suggest. Venom night fighters from No. 23 Squadron were sent towards unidentified radar contacts around Lakenheath, yet the Waterbeach aircrews who were eventually traced did not describe seeing an extraordinary craft or being chased by one. Their evidence points instead to several separate sorties, uncertain radar returns, a possible balloon and a serious aircraft malfunction that complicated the night’s events.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://martinshough.com/aerialphenomena/Lakenheath/brady2.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: martinshough.com">[Martin Shough]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">martinshough.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Martin Shough Archive Index BBC Radio 4 interview with JOHN BRADY</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">JB: I&#x27;m John Brady, at the time of the incident in August 1956 I was a flying officer/navigator on No 23 Night Fighter squadron and at th...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 1956</span></span></span>

The mystery survives because the pilots’ accounts do not fit neatly with the dramatic story recorded by ground controllers and later examined by the United States’ Condon Committee. For [Cambridgeshire]({{ 'what-really-happened-in/' | relative_url }}), the case matters as a rare example of a local RAF station becoming part of a cross-county air-defence incident whose official paperwork, witness memories and radar interpretations still conflict.

## The radar contacts and interception timeline

The wider incident began around RAF Bentwaters in Suffolk on the evening of 13 August. American radar operators reported a series of unusual tracks, including very fast returns, slow-moving groups and a target apparently crossing the station. Bentwaters then contacted RAF Lakenheath, where further radar and visual reports were made. The episode eventually involved American airmen, RAF fighter controllers and aircraft operating across eastern England rather than one continuous object observed from a single place.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File%3AProject_Blue_Book_report_-_1956-08-7069309-Bentwaters-Lakenheath-England.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: commons.wikimedia.org">[wikimedia.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">commons.wikimedia.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Commons File:Project Blue Book report</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wikimedia CommonsFile:Project Blue Book report - 1956-08-7069309-Bentwaters-Lakenheath-England.pdf - Wikimedia CommonsFile:Project Blue B...</span></span></span>

Waterbeach entered the story because No. 23 Squadron was temporarily operating Venom NF.3 night fighters there. These two-seat aircraft carried airborne interception radar: the navigator watched the radar display while the pilot flew headings supplied either by ground control or by the navigator during the final approach. Radar contact did not itself establish what a target was. A return could represent an aircraft, balloon, weather effect, equipment artefact or another reflecting object.

Later reconstruction of the squadron’s records indicates that Waterbeach activity occurred in several distinct phases.

**An early high-altitude contact:** Flying Officer George Sandman and navigator Peter Dell took off on a routine exercise early in the evening. Dell later recalled acquiring a target at extreme range or height while their Venom was already at about 35,000 feet. He believed the object was far above the fighter’s operating ceiling and probably a balloon. The crew landed without making a visual identification.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.martinshough.com/aerialphenomena/Lakenheath/reconstruction.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: martinshough.com">[Martin Shough]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">martinshough.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Martin ShoughA narrative reconstruction of the eventsA narrative reconstruction of the events Archive Index Reconstruction A narrative re...</span></span></span>

**The aborted 21:20 sortie:** Flying Officers Leslie Arthur and Grahame Scofield were scrambled from Waterbeach at about 21:20 GMT. During their climb, both wing-tip fuel tanks were accidentally released, apparently because of an electrical fault. The crew abandoned the sortie and returned to base. Squadron records cited in the later reconstruction state that the tanks were subsequently found in a field near Cambridge. This sortie is significant because some early reports merely said that an aircraft had returned because of a mechanical problem, allowing it to become confused with later interception attempts.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.martinshough.com/aerialphenomena/Lakenheath/reconstruction.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: martinshough.com">[Martin Shough]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">martinshough.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Martin ShoughA narrative reconstruction of the eventsA narrative reconstruction of the events Archive Index Reconstruction A narrative re...</span></span></span>

**The two early-morning interceptions:** Flying Officers David Chambers and John Brady took off at approximately 02:00 on 14 August, followed roughly 40 minutes later by Ian Fraser-Ker and Ivan Logan. Their logbooks therefore place these scrambles substantially later than the interception time implied in some ground-controller accounts. Brady recalled that his Venom was directed towards a target near Lakenheath and obtained intermittent airborne-radar contact, but the crew saw nothing outside the aircraft.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://martinshough.com/aerialphenomena/Lakenheath/brady2.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: martinshough.com">[Martin Shough]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">martinshough.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Martin Shough Archive Index BBC Radio 4 interview with JOHN BRADY</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">JB: I&#x27;m John Brady, at the time of the incident in August 1956 I was a flying officer/navigator on No 23 Night Fighter squadron and at th...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 1956</span></span></span>

This divided timeline is one of the most important findings of the later investigation. The familiar UFO narrative tends to compress the whole night into a single escalating confrontation: strange targets appear, a Waterbeach fighter closes in, the object moves behind it, and another fighter is launched. The squadron evidence suggests a more disorderly sequence containing several aircraft, long gaps, different radar targets and perhaps unrelated causes.

## What Waterbeach aircrews actually reported

The best-known version of the incident came from Technical Sergeant Forrest Perkins, the watch supervisor in Lakenheath’s radar air traffic control centre. Writing years afterwards to the Condon Committee, Perkins described a Venom being guided towards a stationary radar target. He said the pilot reported airborne-radar contact and a weapons-radar lock, after which the unknown target apparently moved behind the fighter and followed it during evasive manoeuvres. RAF controller Freddie Wimbledon later supported the broad outline, stating that the object had been tracked by several ground radars as well as the aircraft.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://files.ncas.org/condon/text/case02.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: files.ncas.org">[ncas.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">files.ncas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Condon Report, Case 2: USAF/RAF Radar Sighting### Case 2</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">#### Summer 1956... &gt; In 1956...(... The identified Waterbeach crews did not confirm this dramatic sequence.

John Brady, navigator in the first early-morning Venom, remembered directing pilot David Chambers towards a radar “paint” or blip. The aircraft made more than one pass, but Brady’s description suggested that the return remained broadly stationary rather than manoeuvring around the fighter. Chambers could not see a corresponding object, and there was no confirmed visual encounter.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://martinshough.com/aerialphenomena/Lakenheath/brady2.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: martinshough.com">[Martin Shough]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">martinshough.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Martin Shough Archive Index BBC Radio 4 interview with JOHN BRADY</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">JB: I&#x27;m John Brady, at the time of the incident in August 1956 I was a flying officer/navigator on No 23 Night Fighter squadron and at th...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 1956</span></span></span>

Ivan Logan, navigator in the second Venom, likewise recalled an unremarkable radar contact rather than an intelligent pursuit. In the later interviews, the crews indicated that the squadron’s working interpretation was a balloon or some other stationary target. Their evidence weakened the strongest element of the classic story: the claim that a clearly detected object deliberately moved behind an RAF fighter and chased it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://martinshough.com/aerialphenomena/Lakenheath/logan.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: martinshough.com">[Martin Shough]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">martinshough.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">I can show you exactly what we said on the interview, because I’ve got a transcript of it.... There is still an awkward complication. Scofield, whose earlier sortie had been aborted when the fuel tanks were released, said he later listened to airborne radio traffic in the Waterbeach crew room. His memory of the transmissions resembled the ground controllers’ account of an exciting interception more closely than the later recollections of the participating crews. He believed he recognised the voices involved, although memories recorded decades after the event cannot settle exact timings or wording.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.martinshough.com/aerialphenomena/Lakenheath/reconstruction.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: martinshough.com">[Martin Shough]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">martinshough.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Martin ShoughA narrative reconstruction of the eventsA narrative reconstruction of the events Archive Index Reconstruction A narrative re...</span></span></span>

Researchers also found that No. 23 Squadron’s commanding officer, Wing Commander A. N. Davis, may have been diverted towards an unknown radar return while flying from RAF Coltishall. This creates the possibility that at least one controller’s description referred to a different aircraft from the two Waterbeach Venoms later assumed to have taken part. No surviving reconstruction has proved that identification, but it shows why matching each radar report to a particular crew remains difficult.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lakenheath-Bentwaters_incident" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikipedia.org.</span></span></span>

## Why the records tell different stories

The contradictions are not simply a choice between truthful witnesses and unreliable ones. Ground controllers and aircrews observed different displays, received different communications and were responsible for different parts of the interception.

A controller could see the fighter and an unidentified return apparently close together on a plan-position radar screen. The aircrew, meanwhile, might receive only a weak or intermittent signal on its own equipment and see nothing visually. Without accurate height data, two returns appearing close on a two-dimensional display might actually be widely separated vertically. A sudden movement could also reflect loss and reacquisition of a return rather than genuine acceleration.

The records were created for different purposes and at different times. The principal American teleprinter message was sent shortly after the incident and preserved the dramatic claim that a target had followed the Venom. Perkins’s detailed account was written more than a decade later for the Condon investigation. The Waterbeach interviews were later still, although the aircrew logbooks provided firmer evidence about take-off times than memory alone.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.martinshough.com/aerialphenomena/Lakenheath/BOI-485.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: martinshough.com">[martinshough.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">martinshough.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on martinshough.com.</span></span></span>

British documentation is less complete than the American Project Blue Book file. The surviving material does not include a full set of original radar plots, recorded radio transmissions or technical data from every radar set. Consequently, later researchers have had to reconstruct the sequence from logbooks, operational messages, interviews and second-hand summaries. The result is more informative than the original popular story, but it cannot reproduce the night minute by minute with certainty.

## Why the case remains disputed

The 1968 Condon Report gave the Lakenheath phase an unusually favourable assessment. Its radar specialist, Gordon D. Thayer, considered anomalous propagation—unusual atmospheric bending of radar energy—but argued that the apparently coherent tracking and reported fighter interaction made a simple propagation explanation difficult. He described it as one of the project’s most puzzling radar-visual cases and judged a physical device to be the most likely explanation, while still allowing for witness error and conventional causes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://files.ncas.org/condon/text/s3chap05.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: files.ncas.org">[files.ncas.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">files.ncas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on ncas.org.</span></span></span>

That conclusion depended heavily on the ground-controller narrative. The Condon investigators did not have the benefit of the later Waterbeach crew interviews or the complete squadron chronology. Once those accounts are included, the strongest interpretation becomes harder to sustain: the pilots did not report a visual object, the alleged chase was not remembered by the identified crews, and at least one radar target appeared stationary and balloon-like.

Sceptical interpretations have proposed several overlapping causes rather than one explanation for the whole night. Possible contributors include anomalous radar propagation, ordinary aircraft, meteors from the Perseid shower and astronomical objects mistaken for lights. These ideas plausibly address parts of the incident, particularly brief visual streaks and some implausibly fast radar estimates, but no single explanation has been demonstrated for every reported contact.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lakenheath-Bentwaters_incident" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on wikipedia.org.</span></span></span>

A balloon is especially relevant to the Waterbeach element because several aircrew statements independently point in that direction. A balloon could appear nearly stationary, remain above a fighter’s practical ceiling or produce a radar return without a conspicuous visual target at night. It does not readily explain every high-speed return reported at Bentwaters or every light seen elsewhere, but those earlier events need not have shared the same cause.

More speculative suggestions include electronic interference, deliberate radar deception or secret military activity. Cold War forces were developing radar countermeasures, and artificial targets could in principle account for behaviour that existed only on radar displays. However, no documentary evidence has emerged showing that a spoofing trial caused the Waterbeach scrambles. It remains a hypothesis rather than an established solution.

## What later research changed

The later investigation both strengthened and weakened the case.

It strengthened the basic historical claim that RAF Waterbeach was genuinely involved. Named crews were found, their logbooks confirmed several sorties, and their accounts showed that unidentified radar returns were taken seriously enough to divert or scramble operational fighters. The incident was not invented by later UFO writers.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.martinshough.com/aerialphenomena/Lakenheath/background.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: martinshough.com">[Martin Shough]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">martinshough.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on martinshough.com.</span></span></span>

At the same time, it weakened the most extraordinary version. The evidence no longer supports treating “the Waterbeach interceptor” as one clearly identified aircraft involved in a continuous radar-and-visual duel. There were several crews, conflicting times and possibly several unrelated targets. The airmen most readily matched to the late scrambles recalled no visible craft, no extraordinary performance and no deliberate pursuit.

The fairest assessment is therefore that RAF Waterbeach encountered a confusing operational situation rather than a confirmed exotic vehicle. Some radar returns remain unidentified because the necessary technical records are missing, not because conventional explanations were eliminated. The case retains genuine historical interest, especially as an example of how radar ambiguity, fragmented communications and later retelling can combine into a powerful UFO narrative.

Within Cambridgeshire’s UFO history, Waterbeach is important precisely because the evidence cuts both ways. It offers unusually strong proof that military personnel responded to unexplained radar contacts, but unusually weak support for the celebrated claim that a mysterious craft outmanoeuvred and chased an RAF fighter.
