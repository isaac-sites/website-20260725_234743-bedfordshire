## Introduction

The 1977 Welsh Triangle was not one continuous encounter but a sighting wave: a succession of reports that gathered around coastal [Pembrokeshire]({{ 'what-really-happened-in-pembrokeshires/' | relative_url }}) after pupils at Broad Haven Primary School described an unfamiliar object near their playground on 4 February. Over the following months, stories emerged from Little Haven, Ripperston and the Marloes peninsula involving bright lights, landed craft, silvery figures and alleged electrical disturbances. Newspapers and television turned these scattered claims into a single regional mystery, variously called the Welsh, Broad Haven or Dyfed Triangle.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://feeds.bbci.co.uk/news/uk-wales-south-west-wales-38723643" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: feeds.bbci.co.uk">[bbci.co.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">feeds.bbci.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">When she visited the field she said there was &quot;two inches of burned grounds&quot;. The wave matters because it shows how a memorable report can become the organising centre of a much larger flap. Some witnesses continued to defend their accounts decades later, and official papers confirm that the reports reached an MP, the Ministry of Defence and RAF personnel. Yet the later incidents were unevenly documented, several plausible hoaxes were admitted or suspected, and no publicly available radar record, photograph or physical trace establishes that one extraordinary phenomenon caused the whole sequence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://discovery.nationalarchives.gov.uk/details/record?catid=8445287&amp;catln=6" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: discovery.nationalarchives.gov.uk">[nationalarchives.gov.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">discovery.nationalarchives.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFO reports and correspondence: April-October 1977 &#124; The National ArchivesUFO reports and correspondence: April-October 1977 &#124; The Nation...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 1977</span></span></span>

## How the reports spread across Pembrokeshire

Broad Haven supplied the wave with its defining image: schoolchildren looking across wet fields and reporting a shiny, elongated object, in some accounts with a dome and flashing light. Researcher David Clarke, drawing on Ministry of Defence records, describes fifteen mainly ten-year-old pupils reporting the object on the ground or partly hidden by vegetation; six reportedly mentioned a tall, silver-clad man nearby. The children’s drawings and the apparent sincerity of individual witnesses made the incident far more newsworthy than an anonymous report of a distant light.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://drdavidclarke.co.uk/page/6/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: drdavidclarke.co.uk">[Dr. David Clarke]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">drdavidclarke.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">David Clarke&#124; Page 6the drawings produced by children in Macclesfield, Cheshire, in 1977 (Credit: crown copyright TNA DEFE 24/1206) Their...</span></span></span>

Publicity followed quickly. Former pupil David Davies later recalled that the arrival of reporters made it difficult to separate reflection on the original sighting from the commotion that followed. National television interviews gave the case an audience far beyond Pembrokeshire, while journalists began connecting new local reports to the school incident. The “triangle” was therefore created as much by storytelling and classification as by geography: separate observations were grouped together because they occurred in roughly the same coastal district and shared a developing vocabulary of silver craft, flashing lights and suited figures.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://feeds.bbci.co.uk/news/uk-wales-south-west-wales-38723643" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: feeds.bbci.co.uk">[BBC Feeds]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">feeds.bbci.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">When she visited the field she said there was &quot;two inches of burned grounds&quot;. The label can also mislead. “Dyfed” was the local-government county established in 1974 and included Pembrokeshire, Carmarthenshire and Cardiganshire. The best-known 1977 cases, however, were concentrated in and around historic Pembrokeshire, particularly the coast facing St Brides Bay. The triangle had no agreed boundary, no official status and no fixed set of qualifying incidents. It was a press-friendly name for a cluster whose edges changed according to the writer telling the story.

A sighting wave does not necessarily mean that witnesses copied one another or invented their experiences. It means that reports became mutually reinforcing. Once residents knew that “UFOs” were being seen locally, an unusual light, aircraft movement or indistinct figure was more likely to be noticed, discussed and reported in the same terms. At the same time, pranksters gained a ready-made script and journalists had an established narrative into which further claims could be placed.

## Little Haven, Marloes and Ripperston claims

### The Little Haven encounter

The strongest adult report associated with the wave came from Rosa Granville, proprietor of the Haven Fort Hotel at Little Haven. In the early hours of 19 April 1977, she said she was disturbed by noise and light and saw an object near the hotel resembling an inverted saucer. Her account included intense heat or coloured light and two tall, apparently featureless figures emerging near the object. She later described disturbed or scorched ground and wrote to Nicholas Edwards, the MP for Pembroke.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nicap.org/chronos/1977fullrep.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nicap.org">[nicap.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nicap.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nicap.org.</span></span></span>

The report received more official attention than the [school sighting]({{ 'school-sighting/' | relative_url }}) because Granville contacted her MP. Edwards told officials that he was being inundated with representations about UFOs in Pembrokeshire. Flight Lieutenant Cowan from nearby RAF Brawdy visited the hotel and inspected the alleged site, but found no evidence that he regarded as proof of a landing. His report considered the possibility of a practical joke and observed that the beings resembled people wearing protective clothing of a kind associated with industrial fires.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://feeds.bbci.co.uk/news/uk-wales-south-west-wales-38723643" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: feeds.bbci.co.uk">[BBC Feeds]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">feeds.bbci.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">When she visited the field she said there was &quot;two inches of burned grounds&quot;. That investigation is significant but easy to overstate. An RAF officer visiting a location shows that a complaint was taken seriously enough to check; it does not mean the RAF accepted that an unknown craft had landed. Cowan’s joking remark that RAF Brawdy would charge normal landing fees if a UFO arrived illustrates the sceptical tone of his assessment. No instrument reading, laboratory result or independently verified ground trace emerged from the visit.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.grunge.com/1395545/1977-ufo-sightings-broad-haven-wales/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: grunge.com">[Grunge]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">grunge.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">1977 ufo sightings broad haven wales</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">1977 ufo sightings broad haven wales</span></span></span>

### Marloes and the widening story

Other reports were placed around the Marloes peninsula, west of Little Haven. Later summaries describe witnesses encountering unusual lights or figures on isolated roads and coastal ground. These accounts extended the story south-westwards and helped turn a Broad Haven incident into a supposed regional pattern.

They are difficult to assess as a coherent body. Many survive chiefly through retrospective journalism, UFO books or retellings rather than complete, time-stamped original statements. Descriptions can also grow more definite with repetition: a light becomes a structured craft, an indistinct person becomes a faceless humanoid, and a local report becomes one of hundreds said to belong to the same wave. The widely repeated total of more than 450 encounters is prominent in recent television-related coverage, but accessible sources do not provide a transparent case list demonstrating that all those reports were independent, contemporaneous and confined to Pembrokeshire.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://unidentifiedphenomena.com/incidents/the-broad-haven-ufo-incident-1977/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: unidentifiedphenomena.com">[Unidentified Phenomena]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">unidentifiedphenomena.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">the broad haven ufo incident 1977</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">the broad haven ufo incident 1977</span></span></span>

### Ripperston Farm

The most elaborate strand centred on the Coombs family at Ripperston Farm, close to the western Pembrokeshire coast. Published accounts attributed a long series of disturbances to the farm: moving lights, tall silver-suited figures, electrical and vehicle failures, scorched areas, frightened animals and cattle allegedly appearing in the wrong fields. These stories later formed the basis of Clive Harold’s *The Uninvited*, first published in 1979.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://books.google.com/books/about/The_Uninvited_The_True_Story_of_Ripperst.html?id=SM-3xQEACAAJ" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: books.google.com">[Google Books]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">books.google.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Books The Uninvited: The True Story of Ripperston Farm</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Books The Uninvited: The True Story of Ripperston Farm</span></span></span>

Ripperston enlarged the wave from aerial sightings into a broader story of repeated intrusion. That makes it culturally important but evidentially weaker. The claims multiplied over time, included phenomena of very different kinds and were reconstructed for a book rather than preserved through a single controlled investigation. A malfunctioning television, a strange light and misplaced livestock do not automatically have a common cause. Nor does the family’s distress establish what caused each reported event.

The Ripperston material therefore needs to be separated into individual claims rather than treated as one cumulative proof. Some observations may have involved aircraft, vehicles, people on the land or ordinary farm problems interpreted during a period of heightened concern. Others remain too vaguely documented to test. The family’s testimony is part of the history of the wave, but the more extraordinary details are not independently verified by the surviving public evidence.

## What officials actually investigated

Ministry of Defence files confirm that UFO correspondence and reports were being collected during 1977. The National Archives holds separate files covering April to October 1977 and October 1977 to March 1978, although catalogue entries alone do not demonstrate that every later claim about the Welsh Triangle appears in them. The department responsible for UFO reports was primarily concerned with possible defence significance, not with proving or disproving extraterrestrial visitation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://discovery.nationalarchives.gov.uk/details/record?catid=8445287&amp;catln=6" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: discovery.nationalarchives.gov.uk">[Discovery]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">discovery.nationalarchives.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFO reports and correspondence: April-October 1977 &#124; The National ArchivesUFO reports and correspondence: April-October 1977 &#124; The Nation...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 1977</span></span></span>

Internal correspondence quoted in later reporting refers to speculation that a practical joker might be operating in the neighbourhood. There was also a request for a discreet inquiry by service police. This combination has produced two contrasting interpretations. Believers see the private inquiry as evidence that officials were more concerned than they admitted publicly. A more restrained reading is that repeated reports near RAF and industrial installations justified checking for hoaxes, trespass, security problems or misunderstood military activity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://feeds.bbci.co.uk/news/uk-wales-south-west-wales-38723643" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: feeds.bbci.co.uk">[BBC Feeds]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">feeds.bbci.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">When she visited the field she said there was &quot;two inches of burned grounds&quot;. RAF Brawdy is relevant because military aircraft and service personnel were a visible part of the local environment. The Milford Haven waterway also contained oil and industrial facilities whose lighting, protective equipment and emergency activity could supply conventional ingredients for some reports. These connections make aviation or industrial explanations plausible, but they do not identify a particular aircraft, exercise or worker as the cause of every sighting.

The official record is therefore less dramatic than later claims of a government cover-up. Officials received reports, corresponded with an MP, inspected at least one site and considered local explanations. What has not emerged is public evidence of radar tracking, an interception, recovered material or an official conclusion that an unknown vehicle penetrated British airspace.

## How publicity can shape a sighting wave

The Welsh Triangle illustrates several processes that can operate at the same time without requiring either mass dishonesty or a single extraordinary cause.

**Attention changes what gets reported.** Before February 1977, a distant light might have been forgotten. After the school story, the same light could be discussed as part of a local mystery. Residents, journalists and investigators were actively looking for related events.

**Witnesses acquire a shared vocabulary.** The Broad Haven accounts established recognisable features: silver surfaces, domes, flashing lights and tall suited figures. Later descriptions using similar language appeared to corroborate the first report, although the witnesses had already been exposed to its imagery.

**The press favours the most vivid cases.** A vague aerial light attracts limited attention. A landed saucer, scorched ground or faceless figure produces a stronger headline. This selection effect can make a wave seem dominated by close encounters even when many underlying reports are much less specific.

**Pranks become more effective.** In 1996, local businessman Glyn Edwards said that he had walked around in a silver suit as a joke during 1977. His admission cannot explain the school object or every figure report, and the precise incidents affected are uncertain. It does show that at least one person exploited the atmosphere surrounding the flap.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://feeds.bbci.co.uk/news/uk-wales-south-west-wales-38723643" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: feeds.bbci.co.uk">[BBC Feeds]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">feeds.bbci.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">When she visited the field she said there was &quot;two inches of burned grounds&quot;. Ms Granville said the incident left her &quot;agitated and d...</span></span></span>

**Memory continues to develop.** Several witnesses have spoken sincerely and consistently about the importance of what happened to them. Long-term sincerity is relevant to whether they knowingly fabricated a story, but it cannot preserve exact distance, duration or sequence over decades. Repeated interviews, documentaries and local commemorations may reinforce a stable narrative without resolving what was originally seen.

The wave should not be dismissed simply as children copying a television programme. Clarke’s archival research notes that groups of schoolchildren elsewhere in Britain reported unusual objects during the same six-month period, while only Broad Haven received sustained national coverage. That comparison suggests that the Welsh case became famous not solely because it was unique, but because the initial report was followed by adult claims, an accessible coastal setting and an unusually durable media story.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://drdavidclarke.co.uk/page/6/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: drdavidclarke.co.uk">[Dr. David Clarke]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">drdavidclarke.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">David Clarke&#124; Page 6the drawings produced by children in Macclesfield, Cheshire, in 1977 (Credit: crown copyright TNA DEFE 24/1206) Their...</span></span></span>

## Did later reporting strengthen the case?

Later coverage strengthened the historical record in a limited sense. It preserved witness recollections, brought the pupils’ drawings back into public view and confirmed that official correspondence and an RAF site visit existed. It also documented the personal cost for witnesses such as David Davies, who said that ridicule and bullying followed him after the incident.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://feeds.bbci.co.uk/news/uk-wales-south-west-wales-38723643" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: feeds.bbci.co.uk">[BBC Feeds]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">feeds.bbci.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">When she visited the field she said there was &quot;two inches of burned grounds&quot;. Ms Granville said the incident left her &quot;agitated and d...</span></span></span>

It did not materially strengthen the physical case. No clear contemporary photograph of the alleged objects has surfaced. No publicly documented radar return connects the incidents. The reported landing traces were not verified as unusual, and the most elaborate Ripperston claims remain dependent on testimony and narrative reconstruction. Modern programmes can gather moving recollections, but they cannot recover measurements that were not taken in 1977.

Nor has one conventional explanation solved the entire wave. A prankster in protective clothing could account for some figure reports but not necessarily an object described by pupils two months earlier. Aircraft or industrial lights could explain some night-time observations but fit poorly with every alleged close encounter. Conversely, the inability to impose one explanation on all reports does not show that they shared an exotic cause. A wave assembled from separate incidents may require separate explanations.

The fairest assessment is that the 1977 Welsh Triangle contains a memorable core of sincerely reported experiences surrounded by weaker, later and increasingly elaborate claims. Broad Haven gave the wave its credibility and publicity; Little Haven brought official attention; Marloes widened its geography; and Ripperston supplied its most dramatic folklore. Together they became the defining episode in Pembrokeshire’s UFO history, but the surviving evidence supports a disputed sighting wave rather than a confirmed visitation.
