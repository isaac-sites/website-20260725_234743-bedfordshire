## Introduction

A UFO report does not automatically “belong” to [County Armagh]({{ 'what-really-flew-over-county-armagh/' | relative_url }}) simply because the word Armagh appears in a police log or newspaper story. The witness may have been standing outside the county, the light may only have appeared in the direction of Armagh, or the location may refer to the modern Armagh City, Banbridge and Craigavon council district, which extends beyond the historic county. Conversely, a report filed under another council area may describe an object seen over historic County Armagh.

For this project, the deciding geography is the historic county shown on the Wikimedia Commons and Wikishire county map. Modern districts, police areas and constituencies remain useful for finding records, but they are not substitutes for that boundary. This distinction matters most around Banbridge, Newry, the Lough Neagh shore and the border with County Monaghan, where place names and sightlines can easily produce misleading classifications.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.proni.gov.uk/proni-historical-maps-viewer" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: proni.gov.uk">[proni.gov.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">proni.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">PRONI Historical Maps Viewer &#124; The Public Record Office of Northern IrelandPRONI Historical Maps Viewer &#124; The Public Record Office o...</span></span></span>

## Historic County Armagh versus modern council areas

Northern Ireland’s six historic counties are still widely used as geographical and cultural names, but they are no longer a tier of local government. The present system consists of 11 local government districts, introduced in April 2015. One of these is Armagh City, Banbridge and Craigavon, whose name combines places from more than one historic county.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nisra.gov.uk/publications/northern-irelands-11-local-government-districts-2015" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nisra.gov.uk">[nisra.gov.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nisra.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Northern Irelands 11 Local Government Districts 2015 &#124; Northern Ireland Statistics and Research AgencyMarch 31, 2016 — # Northern Ir...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 31, 2016</span></span></span>

That creates the first boundary trap. Armagh city, Portadown and Lurgan are straightforward County Armagh locations. Banbridge, however, is historically in County Down, even though it is now in a council and PSNI policing district carrying the Armagh name. A report described administratively as occurring in “Armagh, Banbridge and Craigavon” therefore cannot be counted as a County Armagh sighting until the actual town, road or townland has been identified.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ukga.org/search.php?DB=70&amp;action=ViewRec&amp;recid=16557" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ukga.org">[UK Genealogy Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ukga.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UK Genealogy ArchivesTranscript of the description for BanbridgeTranscript of the description for Banbridge Banbridge BANBRIDGE, a market...</span></span></span>

The opposite problem occurs in the south. Much of historic South Armagh lies within the modern Newry, Mourne and Down district. A report catalogued under that council name may still be an Armagh case if the witness or apparent object location was west of the historic Armagh–Down boundary. Modern administrative labelling can consequently both add false Armagh cases and hide genuine ones.

Historic maps are the safest reference when a location is uncertain. The Public Record Office of Northern Ireland’s map viewer allows users to inspect county, parish and townland boundaries against modern basemaps. Ordnance Survey of Northern Ireland has also released historical County Armagh map series dating from the nineteenth century. These sources are more reliable for classification than assuming that a council, constituency or police-district name describes the historic county.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://admin.opendatani.gov.uk/dataset/osni-open-data-historical-six-inch-to-one-mile-county-series-edition-1-1829-1835-armagh" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: admin.opendatani.gov.uk">[opendatani.gov.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">admin.opendatani.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Open Data NIOSNI Open Data</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open Data NIOSNI Open Data</span></span></span>

### The Newry problem

Newry is the clearest example of why a town name alone may be inadequate. The city developed on both sides of the Clanrye River, which forms part of the historic boundary between Counties Armagh and Down. Newry Town Hall was built on a bridge across the river and is commonly described as symbolising the connection between the two counties. Historical descriptions likewise place portions of Newry parish and settlement in Armagh while locating most of the town in Down.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.open.edu/openlearn/society/politics-policy-people/sociology/newry-frontier-town-or-border-city" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: open.edu">[open.edu]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">open.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-title">The Open University Newry: Frontier town or border city? &#124; Open Learn</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Open University Newry: Frontier town or border city? &#124; Open Learn</span></span></span>

A sighting reported simply as “Newry” should therefore remain geographically unassigned until a more precise location is found. A witness near the western side of the city, or farther into South Armagh, may be in the historic county. Someone east of the river may be in County Down. Even a street address may require checking against a historic boundary layer rather than a modern postcode or council map.

This is not pedantry. If several vague “Newry” reports are automatically added to both county totals, an apparent cluster can be manufactured by duplicate classification. If they are all placed in Down because that is how Newry is popularly described, genuine Armagh observations may disappear from the county record.

## Sightings reported from outside the county

The PSNI’s June 2025 disclosure illustrates a second, different problem. It includes an 8 April 2025 call from Ballymena in which the caller said she could see a UFO “in Armagh”, describing it as a red light in the sky. Ballymena is not in County Armagh. The entry is therefore not an observation made by an Armagh-based witness, although the caller apparently believed that the light was in the direction of Armagh.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.psni.police.uk/foi-disclosure-log/sightings" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: psni.police.uk">[Police Service of Northern Ireland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">psni.police.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on police.uk.</span></span></span>

The published record does not provide the azimuth, elevation, distance estimate, duration or landmarks needed to establish where the light actually was. Nor does it explain whether “Armagh” meant the city, the county, the council district or merely a broad southerly direction. It is best classified as an **Armagh-directed report from outside the county**, not as a confirmed sighting over County Armagh.

A distant witness normally cannot determine an aerial object’s ground position from one line of sight. A light that appears above a particular town or ridge may be much nearer or farther away. Without a second viewpoint, radar range, accurate photographic geometry or another form of triangulation, its true position can remain highly uncertain. Scientific proposals for serious aerial-phenomena investigation therefore emphasise synchronised observations from more than one location, together with calibrated cameras and other sensors.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18566" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on arxiv.org.</span></span></span>

For county-level records, three locations should be kept separate:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Witness location:** where the observer was standing.
* **Apparent direction:** the place, landmark or compass bearing towards which the object was seen.
* **Estimated object location:** where the available evidence suggests the object itself may have been.

</div>

Only the third determines whether the phenomenon was physically over County Armagh, and in many ordinary reports it cannot be established. The first remains important because it determines the viewing angle and which obstructions, flight paths or celestial objects were visible.

This approach avoids two equal errors. It does not reject the Ballymena call as irrelevant, because it may still concern the Armagh sky. But it also does not convert the caller’s impression into a measured aerial position.

## When regional events cross several boundaries

Some unusual lights are genuinely regional events rather than county events. Bright meteors, re-entering space debris, satellite formations and high-altitude aircraft can be visible from widely separated locations. Witnesses in Armagh, Down, Antrim, Tyrone, Monaghan or farther away may all report the same object while assigning it to different parts of the sky.

Fireballs are a useful model. Armagh Observatory and Planetarium describes them as exceptionally bright meteors visible over a large area and encourages observers to submit reports. Its account of the 29 November 2015 fireball noted that the event was captured on video and sought observations from across Ireland rather than treating it as belonging to the county where one witness happened to stand.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://armagh.space/report-a-fireball" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: armagh.space">[Armagh Observatory and Planetarium]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">armagh.space</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on armagh.space.</span></span></span>

A widely seen event in September 2022 produced hundreds of reports from Ireland, Northern Ireland and Scotland. Early descriptions called it a fireball, while later assessment by the UK Meteor Network favoured re-entering space debris. The important geographical lesson is that the same moving light can generate reports across several countries and counties, none of which alone defines its actual path.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.irishtimes.com/ireland/2022/09/15/fireball-spotted-shooting-through-irish-night-sky/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: irishtimes.com">[The Irish Times]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">irishtimes.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on irishtimes.com.</span></span></span>

For an Armagh UFO history, such an event should be included only when Armagh contributes something specific: an observer location, photograph, video, official call, calculated trajectory or useful comparison. It should then be described as a regional event observed from or apparently over Armagh, rather than multiplied into separate unexplained incidents for every place that reported it.

The same caution applies to slow-moving lights. Aircraft approaching Belfast International Airport, commercial traffic on wider routes, drones and satellite passes may be observed from several council and county areas. A light seen from Portadown can continue towards Lough Neagh or County Antrim within minutes. Administrative boundaries have no physical meaning to the object.

## Police geography is not object geography

PSNI material is valuable because it preserves reports that might otherwise be lost, but its systems were designed for policing rather than astronomical reconstruction. Local policing for Armagh, Banbridge and Craigavon covers Armagh city, Banbridge, Lurgan, Portadown and surrounding communities. That operational area crosses the historic Armagh–Down boundary.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.psni.police.uk/about-us/local-policing/armagh-banbridge-and-craigavon?District=ArmaghCityBanbridgeCraigavon" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: psni.police.uk">[Police Service of Northern Ireland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">psni.police.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on police.uk.</span></span></span>

A police location may indicate where a caller lived, where officers would have responded, or which district handled the incident. It does not necessarily indicate the position of the reported object. The PSNI’s published UFO-related logs are brief extracts and generally lack the detailed angles, movement tracks and corroboration required to resolve this ambiguity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.psni.police.uk/foi-disclosure-log/sightings" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: psni.police.uk">[Police Service of Northern Ireland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">psni.police.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on police.uk.</span></span></span>

There is also a retrieval problem. A February 2025 freedom-of-information response said that a search of the PSNI’s NICHE records found 372 incidents containing the term “UFO”, but that manually examining them would exceed the statutory cost limit. A broader June request produced thousands of keyword matches because words such as “alien” or other supplied terms occur in unrelated contexts. The figures cannot be treated as a count of genuine aerial sightings, much less a county-by-county distribution.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.psni.police.uk/foi-disclosure-log/reports-unidentified-phenomena" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: psni.police.uk">[Police Service of Northern Ireland]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">psni.police.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">reports unidentified phenomena</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">reports unidentified phenomena</span></span></span>

This means that apparent differences between Armagh and neighbouring counties may partly reflect record structure, search wording and modern policing geography. A district name in an exported table is not enough to map a case accurately.

## A practical test for counting an Armagh case

A report should be counted as a clear County Armagh case when the witness was within the historic county and the phenomenon was observed locally, or when independent evidence places the object over the county. Reports with less precise geography should be retained, but labelled according to what is actually known.

A useful classification is:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--step-flow" markdown="1">

1. **Confirmed Armagh location:** a named place or coordinates fall within historic County Armagh.
2. **Probable Armagh case:** the report gives a convincing local direction, route or set of landmarks, but the object’s position is not measured.
3. **Armagh-directed report:** the witness was elsewhere and merely described the light as being towards or “in” Armagh.
4. **Modern-district ambiguity:** the source says Armagh City, Banbridge and Craigavon but gives no settlement or townland.
5. **Boundary case:** the location is Newry or another place close to the historic county line and cannot be assigned precisely.
6. **Regional event:** observations from several areas probably concern the same meteor, satellite, aircraft or other moving source.
7. **Non-Armagh case:** the detailed location is outside the historic county, even if the administrative label contains “Armagh”.

</div>

This system preserves uncertain reports without exaggerating the county’s UFO record. It also makes later correction possible. A vaguely located light can move from “boundary case” to “probable Armagh” if a photograph, exact address or second witness is found.

## What the boundary problem changes

The geographical uncertainty weakens any simple claim that County Armagh has a distinct concentration of UFO activity. The available official material is too sparse, and several records cannot be assigned to the historic county from their published wording alone. Modern council and police boundaries introduce further noise by combining Armagh and Down locations under one administrative name.

The boundary problem does not make the reports meaningless. It changes the question from “How many UFOs were seen in Armagh?” to “Which observations can actually be located in relation to historic County Armagh?” That is a more demanding test, but it produces a more honest history.

Under that test, reports from Armagh city, Portadown, Lurgan and well-identified South Armagh locations are generally straightforward. Banbridge should normally be treated as County Down. Newry requires street- or townland-level checking. Reports made from distant places such as Ballymena should be recorded as external viewpoints unless further evidence locates the object. Large moving events should be reconstructed regionally rather than divided into artificial county incidents.

The result is a smaller but clearer Armagh case list: one that distinguishes where a witness stood, where a light appeared and where the evidence suggests it actually travelled.
