## Which Berkshire is being discussed?

This page uses the **historic county of Berkshire**, following the project’s historic-counties map. That distinction matters because modern administrative Berkshire is not identical to the older county. The historic county extends west and north into places now administered as part of Oxfordshire, including Abingdon and much of the Vale of White Horse. Abingdon Town Council confirms that the town was in Berkshire before the 1974 local-government changes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/File%3AEngland_Historic_Counties_Berkshire_map.svg" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[wikipedia.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">File:England Historic Counties Berkshire map.svg</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wikipedia File:England Historic Counties Berkshire map.svg File File history...</span></span></span>

## Windsor’s “UFO” was the Great Meteor of 1783

One of Berkshire’s most striking early sky reports occurred on the evening of 18 August 1783, when a brilliant luminous phenomenon was observed from the terrace of Windsor Castle. Among those present were the natural philosopher Tiberius Cavallo, physician James Lind and the artists Thomas and Paul Sandby. Paul Sandby’s image shows the object in successive stages across the sky, watched by figures on the castle terrace.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://makingscience.royalsociety.org/items/l-and-p_8_50/paper-a-meteor-observed-at-windsor-28-august-1783-by-tiberius-cavallo" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: makingscience.royalsociety.org">[Making Science]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">makingscience.royalsociety.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">paper a meteor observed at windsor 28 august 1783 by tiberius cavallo</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Tiberius Cavallo, Paper, &#x27;A meteor observed at Windsor 28 August 1783&#x27; by Tiberius Cavallo, 21 August 1783, L&amp;P/8/50, The Royal Society A...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: august 1783</span></span></span>

Modern paranormal retellings sometimes present the event as an eighteenth-century UFO that stopped, changed direction or emerged from a cloud. The contemporary scientific record points much more strongly to a bright meteor or fireball. The Royal Society preserved Cavallo’s paper under the description “A meteor observed at Windsor”, and related observations from elsewhere in Britain were published and compared in *Philosophical Transactions*. Another contemporary account described the phenomenon as visible for roughly ten or fifteen seconds with an extended luminous tail.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://makingscience.royalsociety.org/items/l-and-p_8_51/letter-of-the-same-meteor-from-alexander-aubert-to-unknown-recipient" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: makingscience.royalsociety.org">[royalsociety.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">makingscience.royalsociety.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on royalsociety.org.</span></span></span>

The apparent changes of shape and direction are not compelling evidence of controlled flight. A brilliant meteor crossing a large part of the sky can appear to slow, brighten or alter its path because of perspective, fragmentation and the movement of glowing material. The Sandby artwork is historically valuable, but it is a composed representation of several stages of the event rather than a photograph of one object hovering in place.

This case matters because it shows how an unidentified observation can change category as evidence accumulates. The Windsor witnesses saw something extraordinary, documented it carefully and initially lacked a complete physical explanation. Wider reports nevertheless allowed investigators to recognise a large atmospheric meteor rather than a machine of unknown origin. It is therefore best classified as **identified**, not unresolved.

## The 1967 “landings” that fooled police and soldiers

On 4 September 1967, objects resembling small metallic spacecraft were discovered at six sites across southern England. Two were in historic Berkshire: one in a paddock at Winkfield, near Ascot, and another at Welford, west of Newbury. Their placement along an east–west line, strange construction and electronic noises initially suggested a coordinated and potentially serious event. Police cordoned off sites, military and bomb-disposal personnel became involved, and some objects were removed for examination.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1967_British_flying_saucer_hoax" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">A major police and army</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">1967 British flying saucer hoaxThe 1967 British flying saucer hoax was originally thought to be the landing of six mysterious &quot;spaceships...</span></span></span>

The “spacecraft” were fibreglass containers made by apprentices from the Royal Aircraft Establishment at Farnborough as a Rag Week publicity stunt. Some contained electronic sound equipment and a foul-smelling mixture that burst out when investigators drilled into the casing. The perpetrators disclosed the hoax on the day the objects were found.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1967_British_flying_saucer_hoax" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">A major police and army</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">1967 British flying saucer hoaxThe 1967 British flying saucer hoax was originally thought to be the landing of six mysterious &quot;spaceships...</span></span></span>

The Berkshire locations were well chosen. Winkfield lay close to the aviation and defence world around Farnborough, while Welford was in an area associated with military infrastructure and easy access to the A4 corridor. That setting helped make the finds appear plausible and ensured that reports reached official channels quickly.

The episode is important for more than comic effect. It demonstrates that a large emergency response does not authenticate an extraordinary claim. Police, military and technical specialists were responding sensibly to unidentified devices that might have contained explosives or hazardous materials. Their involvement showed uncertainty and caution, not confirmation of alien technology. Once physical examination and information about the makers became available, the case was decisively **debunked**.

## West Berkshire’s local flap stories

The Newbury, Thatcham, Hungerford and Lambourn area has accumulated a substantial body of reported lights, discs, fireballs and cigar-shaped objects. Local retrospective accounts mention an unusual aerial object near Lambourn in 1909, sightings by a Newbury resident during the 1950s and 1960s, oval objects followed by witnesses near Kintbury and Brightwalton in the early 1970s, and recurring orange lights that encouraged organised sky-watching.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.beamsinvestigations.org/Hungerford%20Phenomena.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: beamsinvestigations.org">[Beams Investigations]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">beamsinvestigations.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on beamsinvestigations.org.</span></span></span>

A Newbury-based group, the South-West Aerial Phenomena Society, reportedly formed in 1971 and investigated regional sightings. Its activities included watching for orange lights and interviewing people who described luminous objects or unusual physical sensations. Actress Coral Atkins was among those whose account attracted local interest during this period.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.beamsinvestigations.org/Hungerford%20Phenomena.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: beamsinvestigations.org">[Beams Investigations]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">beamsinvestigations.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on beamsinvestigations.org.</span></span></span>

These stories suggest that [West Berkshire]({{ 'west-berkshire/' | relative_url }}) experienced a modest local “flap”: a period in which publicity, organised investigation and repeated reports reinforced one another. That does not mean witnesses were dishonest. It means that once a subject becomes prominent, more people examine the sky, interpret ambiguous stimuli through the same cultural frame and report experiences that might otherwise have been forgotten.

The evidential difficulty is preservation. Most accessible descriptions are later summaries of newspaper reports rather than original witness forms, photographs, radar records or investigators’ files. Precise dates are sometimes unclear, and descriptions such as “orange lights”, “cigar-shaped structure” or “beams” are not enough to calculate a reliable trajectory or distance. Without the original Newbury press archive and surviving society records, the cluster is best treated as **historically interesting but weakly documented**.

Several ordinary explanations remain plausible across the group rather than for any single report. Bright planets can appear to follow moving observers; meteors and re-entering debris can produce fireballs; aircraft seen head-on may resemble stationary lights; and formations of lanterns or aircraft can appear triangular when no solid structure is visible. Reports involving rapid disappearance are especially vulnerable to cloud cover, viewing angle and loss of visual reference.

## Greenham Common: a powerful setting, thin evidence

Greenham Common carries unusual cultural weight. The former RAF station and United States Air Force base was associated with nuclear weapons, Cold War secrecy and the women’s peace camp. It is therefore a natural setting for stories involving unexplained lights, military aircraft and alleged government concealment.

A much-repeated local report concerns lights seen over the common in September 2006. Witnesses described a triangular or V-shaped formation that appeared to hover before departing, while some accounts linked the sighting with flashes or power disruption. The available online version is a secondary reproduction of local reporting, and no detailed Ministry of Defence, police, electricity-network or air-traffic investigation has been located that verifies all those elements as one connected incident.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ufologie.patrickgross.org/press/newburyweeklynews5oct2006.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufologie.patrickgross.org">[ufologie.patrickgross.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufologie.patrickgross.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on patrickgross.org.</span></span></span>

The triangular description is less conclusive than it sounds. Three or more independent lights naturally create an apparent triangle, especially at night when the observer cannot see whether a dark surface joins them. Aircraft in formation, distant traffic using landing lights and lanterns can all create similar impressions. Claims of exceptional acceleration are also difficult to assess without an established range: a nearby slow object and a distant fast one can produce comparable apparent movement.

Greenham’s military history remains relevant because witnesses may have observed real aviation activity that was unfamiliar, distant or poorly visible. It also increases the risk of narrative inflation: once an unexplained light is attached to a former nuclear base, later retellings may add secrecy, radar confirmation or power effects that were not part of the earliest account. The 2006 sighting should therefore remain **unresolved at a low evidential level**, rather than being treated as evidence of an unknown craft.

## The 2013 Airbus encounter

Berkshire’s most important modern case occurred at 18:35 UTC on 19 July 2013, although early newspaper coverage incorrectly gave the date as 13 July. An Airbus A320 was flying at 34,000 feet approximately 19.5 nautical miles west of Heathrow, near coordinates 51°26′N, 0°58′W. The captain saw an object apparently approaching at the aircraft’s level and believed a collision was imminent. He ducked and attempted to alert the first officer, who had been looking down and did not see it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.airproxboard.org.uk/uploadedFiles/Content/Standard_content/Airprox_report_files/2013/Airprox%20Report%202013086.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: airproxboard.org.uk">[airproxboard.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">airproxboard.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Airprox Report 2013086</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">and pilot report Diagram based on radar dataand pilot report Diagram based on radar data AIRPROX REPORT No 2013086 Date/Time: 19 Jul 2013...</span></span></span>

The captain described the object as bright silver, metallic-looking and shaped like a cigar or rugby ball. He believed it passed within a few feet above the aircraft. There was no impact, and subsequent checks found no Traffic Collision Avoidance System alert. Air traffic control knew of no other aircraft in the immediate area.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.airproxboard.org.uk/uploadedFiles/Content/Standard_content/Airprox_report_files/2013/Airprox%20Report%202013086.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: airproxboard.org.uk">[airproxboard.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">airproxboard.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Airprox Report 2013086</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">and pilot report Diagram based on radar dataand pilot report Diagram based on radar data AIRPROX REPORT No 2013086 Date/Time: 19 Jul 2013...</span></span></span>

This is stronger evidence than a distant light reported from the ground because the witness was an experienced professional, the event was reported through an aviation-safety system and independent technical checks were performed. It nevertheless has important limitations:

* Only the captain saw the object.
* The viewing time was extremely short.
* No radar, camera or instrument detected it.

A small object passing farther away than estimated could have appeared larger and closer because the captain had no stable size reference. Ice, reflective debris, an untracked balloon-like object or an optical effect involving the window cannot be ruled out merely because investigators could not establish one of them. Equally, the report should not be dismissed as invention: the captain’s immediate reaction and formal report indicate that he genuinely perceived a collision threat.

The correct classification is **unresolved**. In aviation terms, that means the available evidence did not identify the hazard. It does not establish extraordinary technology or extraterrestrial origin.

## What official UFO records can and cannot show

British UFO reports were historically handled through the Air Ministry and later the Ministry of Defence. The National Archives holds files covering public reports, official correspondence and defence assessments from the early 1950s through 2009. Relevant series include AIR 2 records from the 1960s and numerous DEFE 24 files from later decades.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://discovery.nationalarchives.gov.uk/details/r/C2645392" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: discovery.nationalarchives.gov.uk">[Discovery]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">discovery.nationalarchives.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nationalarchives.gov.uk.</span></span></span>

These archives are national rather than Berkshire-specific. A report originating in Reading or Newbury may appear among hundreds of chronologically arranged letters, while an aviation incident may instead be preserved by the Civil Aviation Authority or UK Airprox Board. Police logs and local newspaper archives can contain details absent from Ministry of Defence files. Searching only one institution therefore gives an incomplete picture.

Official retention is not the same as official endorsement. The Ministry of Defence recorded reports primarily to assess whether they indicated hostile aircraft, intelligence activity or another threat to UK airspace. Files may preserve sincere testimony without proving the object’s nature. Some records also remain partly or wholly closed because they contain personal information, including reports received during 2009.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://discovery.nationalarchives.gov.uk/details/r/C13442557" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: discovery.nationalarchives.gov.uk">[Discovery]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">discovery.nationalarchives.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nationalarchives.gov.uk.</span></span></span>

The archive’s real value lies in chronology and comparison. Original documents can show whether dramatic details appeared immediately or were added years later, whether separate witnesses were genuinely independent, and whether radar or military involvement was documented rather than merely claimed.

## Why Berkshire produces recurring reports

Berkshire combines several conditions that encourage unusual-sky reports. Heathrow traffic dominates the eastern sky, while smaller aerodromes, military routes and aircraft approaching from the west create complex patterns of lights. The county also contains or adjoins prominent defence sites, including Greenham Common and the Atomic Weapons Establishment at Aldermaston, giving ambiguous sightings an immediate military association.

The Thames Valley is densely populated but contains broad open landscapes such as the Berkshire Downs and former airfield land around Greenham. Observers can see distant lights without being able to judge their size or range. Motorways and railways add moving reflections, while high cloud can hide an aircraft’s body and leave only its lights visible.

Media attention supplies another mechanism. The [1967 hoax]({{ '1967-hoax/' | relative_url }}) generated reports because physical “saucers” had been planted. The West Berkshire flap developed through repeated newspaper coverage and an investigation group. Greenham stories gain significance from the base’s Cold War reputation. These are different processes, but each shows that the social setting influences which observations are noticed, remembered and interpreted as UFOs.

## What remains genuinely unexplained?

The Berkshire record separates into three broad levels.

**Well explained or debunked:** The Windsor object of 1783 belongs to the extensively documented Great Meteor, while the Winkfield and Welford “landings” of 1967 were admitted hoaxes. Neither provides credible evidence of an unknown vehicle.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://makingscience.royalsociety.org/items/l-and-p_8_50/paper-a-meteor-observed-at-windsor-28-august-1783-by-tiberius-cavallo" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: makingscience.royalsociety.org">[royalsociety.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">makingscience.royalsociety.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">paper a meteor observed at windsor 28 august 1783 by tiberius cavallo</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Tiberius Cavallo, Paper, &#x27;A meteor observed at Windsor 28 August 1783&#x27; by Tiberius Cavallo, 21 August 1783, L&amp;P/8/50, The Royal Society A...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: august 1783</span></span></span>

**Unresolved but weakly evidenced:** Many Newbury, Hungerford, Thatcham and Greenham Common reports survive mainly through retrospective newspaper summaries. Their witnesses may have experienced something genuinely puzzling, but the absence of precise contemporary records leaves too many ordinary explanations open.

**Unresolved with a formal investigation:** The 2013 A320 encounter is the county’s strongest case because it generated a detailed aviation report and checks against traffic, radar and balloon information. Those checks narrowed the possibilities but did not establish the object’s distance, size or physical nature.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.airproxboard.org.uk/uploadedFiles/Content/Standard_content/Airprox_report_files/2013/Airprox%20Report%202013086.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: airproxboard.org.uk">[airproxboard.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">airproxboard.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Airprox Report 2013086</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">and pilot report Diagram based on radar dataand pilot report Diagram based on radar data AIRPROX REPORT No 2013086 Date/Time: 19 Jul 2013...</span></span></span>

Berkshire’s UFO history is therefore not a story of accumulating proof. It is a study in how very different events acquire the same label: a natural fireball, manufactured saucers, clusters of ambiguous lights and a pilot’s alarming split-second observation. The most responsible conclusion is that a small residue remains unidentified, chiefly because the available information is insufficient. That is a meaningful finding, but it is not the same as evidence that the objects were extraterrestrial.
