## Introduction

On 4 September 1967, two convincing but entirely artificial “spacecraft” were discovered in [Berkshire]({{ 'what-really-happened-in-berkshires-ufo/' | relative_url }}): one near Welford, west of Newbury, and another at Winkfield, near Ascot. They formed part of a coordinated line of six fake saucers planted across southern England by aircraft-engineering apprentices from the Royal Aircraft Establishment at Farnborough. Police, Ministry of Defence personnel and military specialists responded before physical examination revealed fibreglass shells, ordinary electrical components and fermenting flour paste.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.smithsonianmag.com/history/how-british-college-students-convinced-authorities-that-flying-saucers-were-invading-the-uk-180985442/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: smithsonianmag.com">[Smithsonian Magazine]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">smithsonianmag.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on smithsonianmag.com.</span></span></span>



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Berkshire_fa6d4a_berkshire_1967_hoax_241919-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for 1967 Hoax" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

The incident matters because the official response is sometimes remembered more vividly than the explanation. Authorities really did secure, transport and investigate the objects, but that does not mean they possessed compelling evidence of alien technology. Their caution showed that an unfamiliar object could also be a bomb, hazardous device, fallen aircraft component or hostile equipment. The Berkshire saucers were never an unresolved UFO case: they were deliberately manufactured props whose makers admitted responsibility on the day they were found.

## The objects found at Winkfield and Welford

The Berkshire discoveries were the middle links in an artificial “landing line” running from Clevedon in Somerset to the Isle of Sheppey in Kent. The six principal sites were approximately 30 miles apart: Clevedon, Chippenham, Welford, Winkfield, Bromley and Rushenden. This geographical arrangement was not evidence of an unknown flight path. It had been plotted in advance by the hoaxers, who wanted the finds to look coordinated rather than accidental.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.smithsonianmag.com/history/how-british-college-students-convinced-authorities-that-flying-saucers-were-invading-the-uk-180985442/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: smithsonianmag.com">[Smithsonian Magazine]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">smithsonianmag.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on smithsonianmag.com.</span></span></span>

At Welford, postal worker Eva Rood reportedly encountered a saucer during her morning delivery round. Police removed it to a station, Ministry of Defence officials became involved, and United States Air Force military police from a nearby American facility photographed it. The presence of American personnel occasionally gives later retellings a conspiratorial flavour, but their participation added no evidence that the object was extraterrestrial. It merely reflected the concentration of defence and aviation facilities in and around the Thames Valley during the Cold War.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.smithsonianmag.com/history/how-british-college-students-convinced-authorities-that-flying-saucers-were-invading-the-uk-180985442/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: smithsonianmag.com">[Smithsonian Magazine]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">smithsonianmag.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on smithsonianmag.com.</span></span></span>

The Winkfield object was found in a paddock near Ascot. The location was particularly well chosen because it lay close to a satellite-tracking installation, lending an apparently space-related context to an otherwise unexplained device. Engineer Roger Kenyon reportedly tested the object’s apparent stability by throwing coins at it before it was handed to police. According to contemporary reporting quoted in later accounts, officers placed it in their lost-property office while deciding what to do next.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.smithsonianmag.com/history/how-british-college-students-convinced-authorities-that-flying-saucers-were-invading-the-uk-180985442/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: smithsonianmag.com">[Smithsonian Magazine]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">smithsonianmag.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on smithsonianmag.com.</span></span></span>

Both sites lie within historic Berkshire, the geographical frame used by this project. Their inclusion is therefore not the result of later administrative changes. Together they made Berkshire the only county in which two of the six principal saucers were planted, giving the county a central position in what was otherwise a cross-country operation.

## How the false spacecraft were made

The objects succeeded because they did not look like hastily assembled theatrical props. The apprentices possessed relevant technical skills and spent months preparing them. Work reportedly began in January 1967, when a plaster model was used to mould 12 fibreglass half-shells. These were joined to create six enclosed forms and finished with an aluminium-bearing coating that produced a smooth metallic appearance with few obvious openings or seams.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.smithsonianmag.com/history/how-british-college-students-convinced-authorities-that-flying-saucers-were-invading-the-uk-180985442/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: smithsonianmag.com">[Smithsonian Magazine]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">smithsonianmag.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on smithsonianmag.com.</span></span></span>

Each saucer was roughly 1.4 metres across and weighed about 45 kilograms. A compartment contained a battery-powered loudspeaker, transistor and movement-sensitive switch, allowing the object to make an electronic noise when handled or turned over. The bulk of the interior was filled with flour and water which had been left to ferment into a foul-smelling paste. The hoaxers hoped that anyone opening a saucer might mistake the material for something organic or unfamiliar.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.smithsonianmag.com/history/how-british-college-students-convinced-authorities-that-flying-saucers-were-invading-the-uk-180985442/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: smithsonianmag.com">[Smithsonian Magazine]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">smithsonianmag.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on smithsonianmag.com.</span></span></span>

This combination exploited several expectations at once:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **The metallic exterior** matched the popular image of a flying saucer.
* **The lack of obvious doors or joints** made immediate identification difficult.
* **The unexpected weight** discouraged casual removal.
* **The electronic sound** suggested active machinery rather than an abandoned model.
* **The decomposing paste** created smell, fluid and apparent biological content when the shell was breached.

</div>

The apprentices were not producing flight-capable machines or sophisticated secret technology. They were designing objects that would remain mysterious for long enough to attract police, journalists and specialists. In that limited aim, the construction was highly effective.

## Why the authorities took them seriously

The response was not based solely on a belief in visiting aliens. An unexplained metal object found in a field could represent unexploded ordnance, a surveillance device, military debris or a hazardous container. In 1967, with Cold War defence installations spread across southern England, contacting specialist authorities was a defensible first step. The discovery of several apparently identical objects on the same morning made coordination — whether criminal, military or merely mischievous — a reasonable concern.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.smithsonianmag.com/history/how-british-college-students-convinced-authorities-that-flying-saucers-were-invading-the-uk-180985442/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: smithsonianmag.com">[Smithsonian Magazine]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">smithsonianmag.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on smithsonianmag.com.</span></span></span>

Across the six sites, police forces established cordons, military and Ministry of Defence personnel were consulted, radiation checks were made, and bomb-disposal specialists examined or destroyed objects. At Chippenham, the saucer was blown apart; at Rushenden, an RAF helicopter crew attempting to handle another was sprayed with the malodorous filling when its shell split. One object was reportedly sent for examination at the Atomic Weapons Research Establishment at Aldermaston.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1967_British_flying_saucer_hoax" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">A major police and army</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">1967 British flying saucer hoaxThe 1967 British flying saucer hoax was originally thought to be the landing of six mysterious &quot;spaceships...</span></span></span>

The Berkshire response appears less theatrical than some actions elsewhere. The Welford object was taken into police custody for inspection, while the Winkfield saucer was treated as found property once no immediate danger had been established. Even so, Berkshire Police was reportedly among the forces that considered possible action against the pranksters after the truth emerged. No prosecution followed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.smithsonianmag.com/history/how-british-college-students-convinced-authorities-that-flying-saucers-were-invading-the-uk-180985442/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: smithsonianmag.com">[Smithsonian Magazine]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">smithsonianmag.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on smithsonianmag.com.</span></span></span>

Official files later described the episode as an “obviously very successful” practical joke. They also show that the Ministry of Defence was sensitive about what investigating officers might reveal concerning genuine UFO-reporting procedures, intelligence responsibilities and equipment. An officer involved in examining the Bromley object was reminded not to discuss his other UFO work publicly and, according to archived correspondence, of his obligations under the Official Secrets Act. The secrecy concerned defence procedure, not evidence that the saucers were alien machines.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.theguardian.com/world/2011/mar/03/alien-invasion-hoax-fooled-ministry" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theguardian.com">[The Guardian]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theguardian.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Whitehall papers released at the National Archives show that within Whitehall the &quot;1967 flying saucer hoax&quot; was regarded at the MoD a...</span></span></span>

## How physical examination exposed the hoax

The decisive evidence came from the objects themselves. Their appearance generated the mystery, but opening them destroyed it. Engineers and military specialists found conventional construction materials, British commercial batteries, simple wiring and loudspeaker equipment. The unpleasant interior substance proved to be decaying flour paste rather than fuel, biological tissue or an unknown chemical.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.smithsonianmag.com/history/how-british-college-students-convinced-authorities-that-flying-saucers-were-invading-the-uk-180985442/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: smithsonianmag.com">[Smithsonian Magazine]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">smithsonianmag.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on smithsonianmag.com.</span></span></span>

These findings established a straightforward chain of explanation:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--step-flow" markdown="1">

1. The shells had been moulded and coated by people familiar with fibreglass fabrication.
2. The sounds came from a basic electrical circuit activated by movement.
3. The supposed internal matter was an ordinary food mixture allowed to spoil.
4. The six matching objects had been deliberately positioned at measured intervals.
5. Their makers publicly admitted constructing and planting them.

</div>

The perpetrators were apprentices associated with the Royal Aircraft Establishment, a major British aeronautical research centre at Farnborough. Chris Southall and Roger Palmer are generally identified as the leading organisers, supported by teams who transported the objects to the chosen sites during the early hours. Camping equipment provided a cover story should anyone stop them on the road.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.smithsonianmag.com/history/how-british-college-students-convinced-authorities-that-flying-saucers-were-invading-the-uk-180985442/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: smithsonianmag.com">[Smithsonian Magazine]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">smithsonianmag.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on smithsonianmag.com.</span></span></span>

Their purpose was publicity for Rag Week, a programme of charitable fundraising stunts. The group also said it had wanted to test how authorities might respond to an apparent landing. A journalist familiar with earlier Farnborough apprentice pranks contacted the establishment that evening, and the apprentices admitted responsibility roughly 12 hours after the first discovery. The resulting publicity helped their fundraising reach a reported £2,000.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.smithsonianmag.com/history/how-british-college-students-convinced-authorities-that-flying-saucers-were-invading-the-uk-180985442/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: smithsonianmag.com">[Smithsonian Magazine]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">smithsonianmag.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on smithsonianmag.com.</span></span></span>

## What the Berkshire case does — and does not — prove

The hoax is sometimes presented as evidence that British officials panicked at the mere sight of a saucer. That interpretation is too simple. Police and military teams faced solid, heavy, unidentified devices whose contents and purpose were unknown. Initial caution was appropriate. The more revealing weakness was inconsistent handling: some objects were probed, tasted, transported or broken open before their contents had been safely identified. The episode exposed shortcomings in coordination and hazardous-object procedure more clearly than it exposed official belief in extraterrestrials.

It also illustrates why **official attention is not confirmation**. Governments routinely investigate uncertain reports because of their possible defence, aviation or public-safety implications. An RAF helicopter, bomb-disposal unit or intelligence officer at a scene demonstrates that an object had not yet been identified; it does not establish that extraordinary claims about it were judged credible.

Physical evidence is not automatically reliable either. The Berkshire saucers were tangible, could be photographed, emitted sounds and contained unusual-looking material. Yet every one of those features had been engineered to mislead. Useful physical evidence must have a secure history, be examined under controlled conditions and withstand ordinary technical testing. These objects failed as soon as their construction and components were exposed.

Later reporting has strengthened the hoax explanation rather than weakened it. Surviving press coverage, official correspondence, photographs, witness recollections and accounts from the makers agree on the central sequence. Minor details differ between retellings — including the apprentices’ precise ages, which object went to which establishment and occasional references to a separate “seventh saucer” in central London — but none creates doubt about the origins of the Welford and Winkfield devices. The six planned saucers were manufactured at Farnborough and deliberately planted.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.smithsonianmag.com/history/how-british-college-students-convinced-authorities-that-flying-saucers-were-invading-the-uk-180985442/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: smithsonianmag.com">[Smithsonian Magazine]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">smithsonianmag.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on smithsonianmag.com.</span></span></span>

## Berkshire’s lasting lesson

Within Berkshire’s UFO history, the 1967 episode is important precisely because it is solved. Two apparent landed spacecraft produced real witnesses, real objects, real police records and real military interest, yet the explanation was an organised technical prank. The case provides a useful benchmark for assessing less well-documented local reports: institutional involvement can show that authorities responded, but only the quality of the surviving evidence can show what actually happened.

At Welford and Winkfield, that evidence is unusually complete. The objects’ materials were identified, their mechanism reconstructed, their placement explained and their builders named. The Berkshire flying saucers were not unexplained craft that officials later dismissed. They were false spacecraft whose convincing design briefly delayed an ordinary conclusion.

    params.push('toolid=' + encodeURIComponent(cfg.tool_id || '10001'));
    if (reference) params.push('customid=' + encodeURIComponent(reference).slice(0, 256));
    return url + joiner + params.join('&');
  }
  function domainLabel(marketId) {
    var cfg = markets[marketId] || markets[defaultMarket] || {};
    return cfg.domain_label || 'eBay';
  }
  function flagClass(marketId) {
    return 'fr-ebay-market-flag fr-ebay-market-flag--' + String(marketId || '').toLowerCase().replace(/_/g, '-').replace(/[^a-z0-9-]+/g, '');
  }
  function closeMenu(section) {
    var menu = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-menu]');
    var trigger = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-trigger]');
    var picker = section.querySelector('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
    if (menu) menu.hidden = true;
    if (trigger) trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', 'false');
    if (picker) picker.classList.remove('fr-ebay-market-picker--open');
  }
  function availableMarkets(section) {
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      return Array.prototype.slice.call(select.options)
        .map(function (option) { return option.value; })
        .filter(function (marketId) { return !!markets[marketId]; });
    }
    return Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]'))
      .map(function (button) { return button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'); })
      .filter(function (marketId) { return !!markets[marketId]; });
  }
  function applyMarket(section, marketId, persist) {
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    if (available.indexOf(marketId)< 0) marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-localized-link]')).forEach(function (link) {
      var query = link.getAttribute('data-ebay-query') || '';
      var reference = link.getAttribute('data-ebay-reference') || '';
      link.setAttribute('href', buildUrl(query, reference, marketId));
    });
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select && available.indexOf(marketId) >= 0) select.value = marketId;
    var selectedText = marketId;
    if (select && select.selectedIndex >= 0) selectedText = select.options[select.selectedIndex].text;
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-selected-market-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-selected-market-flag], [data-ebay-trigger-market-flag]')).forEach(function (flag) {
      flag.className = flagClass(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-trigger-market-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = selectedText;
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-domain-label]')).forEach(function (label) {
      label.textContent = domainLabel(marketId);
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.setAttribute('aria-selected', button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option') === marketId ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      var active = button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button') === marketId;
      button.setAttribute('aria-pressed', active ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-selected-market', marketId);
    if (persist) {
      try { if (window.localStorage) localStorage.setItem('phoenix-ebay-market', marketId); } catch (err) {}
    }
    closeMenu(section);
  }
  sections.forEach(function (section) {
    if (section.getAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init') === '1') return;
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init', '1');
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    var marketId = defaultMarket;
    if (window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation && window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket) {
      marketId = window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket(available, {
        storageKey: 'phoenix-ebay-market',
        defaultMarket: defaultMarket
      });
    } else if (available.indexOf(defaultMarket)< 0) {
      marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    }
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      select.addEventListener('change', function () {
        applyMarket(section, select.value, true);
      });
    }
    var trigger = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-trigger]');
    var menu = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-menu]');
    if (trigger && menu) {
      trigger.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
        event.stopPropagation();
        var expanded = trigger.getAttribute('aria-expanded') === 'true';
        menu.hidden = expanded;
        trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', expanded ? 'false' : 'true');
        var picker = trigger.closest('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
        if (picker) picker.classList.toggle('fr-ebay-market-picker--open', !expanded);
      });
      Array.prototype.slice.call(menu.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
        button.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
          event.stopPropagation();
          applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option'), true);
        });
      });
      document.addEventListener('click', function () { closeMenu(section); });
    }
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.addEventListener('click', function () {
        applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'), true);
      });
    });
    applyMarket(section, marketId, false);
  });
})();
