## Introduction

Heathrow’s UFO history is closely tied to aviation safety rather than to claims of confirmed extraordinary craft. Reports associated with the airport matter because an unidentified object near a major flight corridor has a practical question behind it: could it threaten aircraft? The most significant Heathrow-related cases are therefore not simply unusual sightings, but incidents where pilots, air traffic services or investigators had to consider whether an unknown object was a hazard.

The best-known example is a 1991 report involving an Alitalia aircraft approaching Heathrow, in which pilots reported seeing a missile-shaped object close to their flight path. The case remains interesting because it combined a professional aviation witness account with questions about radar confirmation and identification, but it did not produce evidence that the object was an unknown technology. ## The 1991 aircraft near-miss report

### What the pilots reported

On 21 April 1991, an Alitalia MD-80 crew approaching London Heathrow reported seeing an unusual object during descent. According to compiled aircraft-encounter records, Captain Achille Zaghetti reported a short-duration sighting of a brown, missile-shaped object that appeared to cross the aircraft’s path. The object was estimated by the crew to be above the aircraft, and the report recorded that the co-pilot also saw it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://project1947.com/47cats/acufoe_dw.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: project1947.com">[project1947.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">project1947.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">acufoe dw</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">acufoe dw</span></span></span>

The case gained attention because pilot observations are often considered more significant than ordinary ground sightings. Airline crews are trained observers operating in an environment where distance, speed and relative movement are central to their work. However, aviation investigators also recognise that a brief visual encounter from a moving aircraft can be difficult to interpret. A pilot may accurately report that something was present while still lacking enough information to identify what it was.

The strongest part of the report is therefore the existence of a professional witness account. The weakest part is the lack of publicly available physical evidence that would determine the object’s exact nature. There was no publicly confirmed recovered object, image record or independent proof that the object represented anything beyond an unidentified aerial observation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://project1947.com/47cats/acufoe_dw.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: project1947.com">[project1947.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">project1947.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">acufoe dw</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">acufoe dw</span></span></span>

### Why radar questions matter

Radar evidence is often treated as a deciding factor in UFO discussions, but aviation systems are designed for particular purposes rather than for identifying every object in the sky. A radar return may provide position information, while a small object, unusual angle or limited tracking information may complicate identification.

In the Heathrow 1991 report, radar questions became part of the debate because an object near an aircraft would normally raise interest among aviation authorities. Yet an absence of a publicly available radar confirmation does not prove that nothing was seen; it means the available evidence does not establish what the object was. This distinction is important in aviation-related UFO cases: an incident can be real as a report while remaining unresolved as an explanation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://project1947.com/47cats/acufoe_dw.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: project1947.com">[project1947.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">project1947.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">acufoe dw</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">acufoe dw</span></span></span>

## Pilot observations and radar questions

Heathrow’s importance in UFO reporting comes from its position as a highly controlled aviation environment. The airport sits beneath dense arrival and departure routes, meaning unusual observations are often filtered through existing safety systems. A report near Heathrow is therefore treated first as an aviation issue, not as a mystery story.

The modern approach to unidentified aerial reports around Heathrow is visible in UK Airprox investigations. The UK Airprox Board examines incidents where aircraft crews report possible risks from unknown objects, including drones, balloons and other airborne hazards. These investigations focus on separation distances, radar information, pilot testimony and the level of risk to flight safety.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.airproxboard.org.uk/Documents/Download/1718/bd8a91f8-55d7-4573-9ba6-f8272a7c55a2/1149" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: airproxboard.org.uk">[airproxboard.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">airproxboard.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">AIRPROX REPORT No 2014117...</span></span></span>

Examples from the Heathrow area show why unidentified objects can create concern even when there is no evidence of anything unusual. In 2014, an A320 crew approaching Heathrow reported a small black object passing very close to the aircraft, with the pilot believing it may have been a small radio-controlled helicopter. The report was investigated as an Airprox event, with the focus on the possible collision risk rather than the object’s origin.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.airproxboard.org.uk/Documents/Download/1718/bd8a91f8-55d7-4573-9ba6-f8272a7c55a2/1149" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: airproxboard.org.uk">[airproxboard.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">airproxboard.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">AIRPROX REPORT No 2014117...</span></span></span>

## Aviation safety versus mystery claims

The main significance of Heathrow UFO reports is the way they expose the difference between a strange observation and an aviation threat. A pilot seeing an unknown object near an aircraft is a serious safety matter even when the eventual explanation is ordinary. A drone, balloon, model aircraft or misidentified object can still create danger because of collision risk.

This is why aviation authorities generally avoid the question “what kind of mystery was this?” and instead ask practical questions:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--comparison" markdown="1">

* Was the object close enough to threaten the aircraft?
* Was there evidence from radar, air traffic control or other witnesses?
* Could it be matched to known traffic, weather activity or permitted operations?
* Did the event require changes to procedures or warnings to other crews?

</div>

The distinction can be seen in Heathrow-related drone and unknown-object reports. Investigators have sometimes classified events as involving a genuine safety concern while still being unable to identify the object conclusively. In one Heathrow-area Airprox review, investigators noted that a reported object could not be confirmed because supporting evidence was limited, even though the pilot’s account required a safety assessment.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.airproxboard.org.uk/reports-and-analysis/monthly-airprox-reviews/airprox-reports-2023/july/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: airproxboard.org.uk">[airproxboard.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">airproxboard.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Monthly meeting July 2023 &#124; UK Airprox Board...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 2023</span></span></span>

For UFO researchers, Heathrow cases are valuable because they sit at the boundary between public curiosity and professional investigation. They show that aviation reports are often among the better documented UFO accounts, but documentation of a sighting is not the same as proof of an unusual craft. The strongest conclusion from Heathrow’s record is not that unknown objects are confirmed extraordinary vehicles; it is that busy airspace creates a need for careful reporting whenever pilots encounter something they cannot immediately identify.
