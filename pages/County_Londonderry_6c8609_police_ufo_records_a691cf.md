## Introduction

Police records provide the strongest official paper trail for UFO claims in [County Londonderry]({{ 'what-really-flew-over-county-londonderry/' | relative_url }}), but they do not establish that unexplained craft were present. The key Police Service of Northern Ireland disclosure contains three local entries: one sighting that the caller identified as an aeroplane during the call, one confused conversation mentioning a UFO alongside cars, and one unsupported expectation that a UFO would land in a garden. None includes photographs, radar data, corroborating witnesses or a documented aviation investigation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.psni.police.uk/sites/default/files/2022-10/01915%20-%20Unidentified%20Flying%20Object%20%28UFO%29%20or%20Unidentified%20Aerial%20Phenomena.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: psni.police.uk">[PSNI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">psni.police.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on police.uk.</span></span></span>

Their real value is evidential rather than sensational. The logs confirm that people contacted the police and preserve brief summaries of what was said. They also show why a police record should not automatically be treated as a police-verified sighting. The entries are calls for service retrieved through keyword searches, not completed case files demonstrating objects that remained unidentified after investigation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.psni.police.uk/sites/default/files/2022-10/01915%20-%20Unidentified%20Flying%20Object%20%28UFO%29%20or%20Unidentified%20Aerial%20Phenomena.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: psni.police.uk">[PSNI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">psni.police.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on police.uk.</span></span></span>

## What the PSNI disclosure actually contains

The principal document is freedom-of-information response F-2022-01915. It sought correspondence mentioning UFOs, unidentified aerial phenomena, lights in the sky, aliens or extraterrestrial beings between May 2016 and the end of 2019. The PSNI returned nine Northern Ireland entries, three of them at addresses in Londonderry.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.psni.police.uk/sites/default/files/2022-10/01915%20-%20Unidentified%20Flying%20Object%20%28UFO%29%20or%20Unidentified%20Aerial%20Phenomena.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: psni.police.uk">[PSNI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">psni.police.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on police.uk.</span></span></span>

These are unusually revealing because the summaries include information that disappears when the cases are reduced to headlines or map markers. “Three UFO reports in Londonderry” is technically defensible as a description of three keyword hits, but it creates a much stronger impression than the underlying records justify.

The disclosure itself warns that the results came solely from a keyword search. Their completeness depended on which words had been entered into police systems, and the manual extraction process allowed for human error. The PSNI added that other relevant calls might exist but could not be found without reviewing every report made to police.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.psni.police.uk/sites/default/files/2022-10/01915%20-%20Unidentified%20Flying%20Object%20%28UFO%29%20or%20Unidentified%20Aerial%20Phenomena.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: psni.police.uk">[PSNI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">psni.police.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on police.uk.</span></span></span>

## The Strand Road sighting resolved as an aeroplane

The Strand Road entry is the most apparently dramatic of the three. Read only as “metallic spinning disk flying above a building”, it resembles a classic daylight flying-saucer report. The decisive information follows immediately: the caller clarified that the object was a plane.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.psni.police.uk/sites/default/files/2022-10/01915%20-%20Unidentified%20Flying%20Object%20%28UFO%29%20or%20Unidentified%20Aerial%20Phenomena.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: psni.police.uk">[PSNI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">psni.police.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on police.uk.</span></span></span>

This makes Strand Road a resolved misidentification, not an unexplained incident. There is no need to speculate about an exotic object that happened to resemble an aircraft, because the surviving record says the witness supplied the identification. Nor is there evidence that police officers separately observed the object, checked radar, contacted an airport or opened an investigation into an airspace intrusion.

The case illustrates how descriptions can become distorted when detached from their outcome. A search result, spreadsheet or newspaper paragraph may preserve the striking opening phrase while giving less prominence to the mundane resolution. In UFO research, that difference is fundamental. An object can be unidentified for a few seconds and then identified without ever becoming a genuine unexplained case.

It also shows why shape words require caution. A distant aeroplane seen at an unusual angle can present a compact or apparently disc-like outline, particularly when its wings are difficult to distinguish. Glare, reflections and changes in bank angle may alter its apparent shape or produce an impression of spinning. The police log does not provide enough observational detail to reconstruct the exact visual effect, but it does provide the outcome that matters: the caller recognised a plane.

The strongest conclusion is therefore modest but firm. Something was seen over Strand Road; it was initially described in extraordinary terms; and it was resolved within the same conversation. The record supports perception followed by identification, not an aircraft-defying mystery.

## Why the Holly Lane call is not a usable sighting report

The Holly Lane entry contains the word “UFO”, but almost none of the information required to assess an aerial observation. It gives no time of sighting, direction, altitude, apparent size, colour, duration, movement, weather conditions or number of witnesses. Instead, the police summary places the UFO reference among remarks about a pink Ferrari and a 1967 Mustang.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.psni.police.uk/sites/default/files/2022-10/01915%20-%20Unidentified%20Flying%20Object%20%28UFO%29%20or%20Unidentified%20Aerial%20Phenomena.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: psni.police.uk">[PSNI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">psni.police.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on police.uk.</span></span></span>

That does not prove the caller had seen nothing. It means the disclosed record cannot support a responsible conclusion either way. There is no coherent claim to test against aircraft movements, planets, satellites, drones or atmospheric effects. Treating the entry as a conventional UFO sighting would silently convert a fragment of confused speech into an organised eyewitness account.

The distinction matters because official status can lend weak material an undeserved sense of authority. The PSNI recorded the communication because someone contacted the police, not because officers had endorsed every statement made during it. Police control-room logs routinely preserve allegations, impressions, mistaken beliefs and incomplete information so that calls can be assessed and managed. Their existence verifies the report, not the reported event.

It would also be inappropriate to make retrospective claims about the caller’s health, intentions or reliability from this short entry. The record allows only a narrow assessment: the reference is too confused and poorly specified to function as evidence of an unidentified object over Londonderry.

## What can be learned from the Edenreagh Road call?

The Edenreagh Road record is similarly weak, although for a different reason. The caller said he was waiting for a UFO to land in his garden, and the summary ends with “no other details”. It does not say that an object was visible, that lights were approaching, that anything had entered the garden or that another person witnessed an event.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.psni.police.uk/sites/default/files/2022-10/01915%20-%20Unidentified%20Flying%20Object%20%28UFO%29%20or%20Unidentified%20Aerial%20Phenomena.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: psni.police.uk">[PSNI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">psni.police.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on police.uk.</span></span></span>

Grammatically, the statement concerns an expected future occurrence rather than a completed observation. It may have arisen from a belief, a joke, a misunderstanding or circumstances omitted from the short disclosure. The record cannot distinguish among those possibilities.

This is an important failure mode in keyword datasets. A search for “UFO” retrieves any record containing the term, regardless of whether it describes:

* or confused speech in which the keyword has little evidential importance.

</div>

The Edenreagh Road call therefore proves only that a caller made the recorded statement. It does not establish a sighting, let alone an unexplained aerial object.

## Why keyword totals exaggerate the evidence

Later PSNI disclosures show how dramatically totals can change according to the search method. A 2022 response concerning a much longer period said that 206 police serials since May 2019 matched broad criteria including “UFOs”, “UAPs”, “lights in sky”, “aliens” and “extra-terrestrial”. The PSNI did not present those 206 records as verified sightings. It explained that each would require manual examination to determine whether it was relevant.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.psni.police.uk/sites/default/files/2022-09/01566%20UFO-UAP%20Sightings.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: psni.police.uk">[PSNI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">psni.police.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on police.uk.</span></span></span>

A separate 2025 response reported 372 incidents containing the term “UFO” on the PSNI’s NICHE records system. Again, the authority said the records were not held in a readily retrievable format and that each incident would need to be reviewed manually. The broad request was refused because this work was estimated to exceed the Freedom of Information Act cost limit.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.psni.police.uk/foi-disclosure-log/unidentified-flying-objects-ufos-or-unidentified-aerial-phenomena-uaps" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: psni.police.uk">[PSNI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">psni.police.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">unidentified flying objects ufos or unidentified aerial phenomena uaps</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Unidentified Flying Objects (UFOs) or Unidentified Aerial Phenomena (UAPs) &#124; PSNIFebruary 21, 2025 — Unidentified Flying Objects (UFO...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 21, 2025</span></span></span>

Neither figure should be interpreted as 206 or 372 aerial mysteries. The smaller nine-entry disclosure demonstrates why. Its results included a plane, a description matching a drone, callers who supplied no useful details and conversations in which the UFO reference was muddled or incidental.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.psni.police.uk/sites/default/files/2022-10/01915%20-%20Unidentified%20Flying%20Object%20%28UFO%29%20or%20Unidentified%20Aerial%20Phenomena.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: psni.police.uk">[PSNI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">psni.police.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on police.uk.</span></span></span>

The apparent discrepancy between nine reports in one period, 206 broad search hits after May 2019 and 372 NICHE references over a later ten-year request is not evidence of a hidden wave. The requests used different date ranges, systems, terms and inclusion rules. Some searched for “lights in the sky” and “aliens” as well as UFOs; others searched a single keyword. Until individual records are reviewed and duplicates or irrelevant uses removed, the totals measure database matches rather than sighting quality.

Recent PSNI releases continue to show the same pattern. Logs published for 2024 and early 2025 include ordinary witness descriptions, reports made months after an event and a Coleraine 999 call with no direct request beyond the word “UFO”. The entries preserve what callers communicated, but the release does not turn them into investigated or confirmed anomalies.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.psni.police.uk/foi-disclosure-log/sightings" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: psni.police.uk">[PSNI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">psni.police.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Sightings &#124; PSNIJune 23, 2025 — Sightings &#124; PSNI Home / FOI Disclosure Log / Sightings Sightings June 23, 2025 &#124; Incident and Crime S...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 23, 2025</span></span></span>

## What the police records prove — and what they do not

For County Londonderry, the records support several limited conclusions.

**They prove that UFO language reached the police.** At least three local communications containing the term or concept were recorded between 2016 and 2019. Because the PSNI warns that its search may not have found every relevant call, three should be treated as a recovered minimum for that particular search method, not a definitive total of everything reported in the historic county.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.psni.police.uk/sites/default/files/2022-10/01915%20-%20Unidentified%20Flying%20Object%20%28UFO%29%20or%20Unidentified%20Aerial%20Phenomena.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: psni.police.uk">[PSNI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">psni.police.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on police.uk.</span></span></span>

**They preserve useful contemporary wording.** Unlike stories submitted years later to a UFO website, these summaries were created within a police-recording process. That gives them value as evidence of what was communicated at or near the time of contact, although they remain compressed summaries rather than verbatim witness statements.

**They reveal how weak reports enter official statistics.** One local case was resolved as an aeroplane; the other two lacked coherent observational evidence. The dataset is therefore a direct warning against equating “recorded by police” with “unexplained by police”.

What the records do **not** prove is equally important. They do not show that officers witnessed unusual craft, that aircraft were tracked on radar, that air-traffic controllers reported unidentified traffic, that the Ministry of Defence or Royal Air Force investigated the Londonderry calls, or that any object demonstrated extraordinary speed, manoeuvrability or technology. The published table records no physical evidence and no unresolved police finding.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.psni.police.uk/sites/default/files/2022-10/01915%20-%20Unidentified%20Flying%20Object%20%28UFO%29%20or%20Unidentified%20Aerial%20Phenomena.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: psni.police.uk">[PSNI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">psni.police.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on police.uk.</span></span></span>

The distinction is consistent with the wider history of British official UFO records. The Ministry of Defence formerly collected reports, many of which are now publicly archived, but its files were primarily concerned with possible defence implications. The department closed its UFO desk in 2009 after concluding that decades of reports had revealed neither an extraterrestrial presence nor a military threat requiring the desk’s continuation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/ufo-reports-in-the-uk" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: GOV.UK">[gov.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">GOV.UK</span><span class="citation-popover-title">From: Ministry of Defence</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFO reports in the UK - GOV.UKDecember 4, 2007 — UFO reports in the UK - GOV.UK Home Defence and armed forces FOI release UFO reports in...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 4, 2007</span></span></span>

That national conclusion does not automatically explain every local observation. It clarifies what an official record means: governments and police may record unusual claims for administrative, safety or intelligence reasons without certifying the claims as physically real.

## A practical test for reading future police disclosures

A police UFO entry becomes significantly more useful when it includes enough information for independent checking. Readers should look for an exact time and viewing location; a direction and elevation; duration and movement; weather and visibility; independent witnesses; original photographs or video with metadata; and documented checks against aircraft, drones, satellites, astronomical objects or emergency activity.

Evidence is stronger still where police officers, pilots or air-traffic personnel made independent observations, where radar or calibrated instruments recorded a corresponding object, or where investigators documented why ordinary explanations failed. None of those features appears in the three disclosed Londonderry entries.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.psni.police.uk/sites/default/files/2022-10/01915%20-%20Unidentified%20Flying%20Object%20%28UFO%29%20or%20Unidentified%20Aerial%20Phenomena.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: psni.police.uk">[PSNI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">psni.police.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on police.uk.</span></span></span>

The fairest classification is therefore straightforward:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Strand Road:** resolved as an aeroplane.
* **Holly Lane:** confused and unusable as an aerial sighting report.
* **Edenreagh Road:** an unsupported statement, with no recorded observation to assess.

</div>

County Londonderry’s police logs matter because they expose the gap between an official record and official verification. They are authentic records of calls, but weak evidence for unusual objects. Far from confirming a hidden mystery, the surviving details show how ordinary identification, ambiguous language and poor information can accumulate beneath the impressive label of “police UFO reports”.
