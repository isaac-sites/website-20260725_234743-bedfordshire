## Introduction

RAF Brawdy makes military activity one of the strongest conventional explanations for at least some of [Pembrokeshire]({{ 'what-really-happened-in-pembrokeshires/' | relative_url }})’s 1977 UFO reports. The station was not a quiet reserve airfield: it housed the RAF’s No. 1 Tactical Weapons Unit, where pilots trained in low-level navigation, air combat and weapons delivery using Hawker Hunter jets. An adjoining British-American naval facility was also operating as part of a highly secret Cold War submarine-detection network.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.rafmuseum.org.uk/research/online-exhibitions/taking-flight/historical-periods/modern-flying-training-1970-present/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: rafmuseum.org.uk">[rafmuseum.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">rafmuseum.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">modern flying training 1970 present</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">During the 1970s the low-level tactical strike role of the RAF was developed.... Consequently, a Tactical Weapons Unit (TWU) was formed...</span></span></span>

That setting matters, but it does not settle the case. Military aircraft could account for unfamiliar shapes, sudden appearances, loud or oddly distant sounds and errors in judging speed or scale. Service clothing may also have influenced reports of silver-suited figures. Yet no released record identifies a particular Brawdy flight as the cause of the [Broad Haven school]({{ 'school-sighting/' | relative_url }}) sighting or the more dramatic encounters reported elsewhere. The military connection is therefore a plausible mechanism, not proof of either an official cover-up or a complete explanation.

## RAF Brawdy’s defence role in 1977

The RAF returned to Brawdy in 1974, only a few years after the Royal Navy had withdrawn from operating the airfield. No. 229 Operational Conversion Unit moved there from RAF Chivenor and was reorganised as No. 1 Tactical Weapons Unit. The RAF Museum describes the unit as part of the expansion of low-level tactical strike training during the 1970s: Brawdy was specifically used to teach pilots the skills required for fast, low-level military flying.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.rafmuseum.org.uk/research/online-exhibitions/taking-flight/historical-periods/modern-flying-training-1970-present/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: rafmuseum.org.uk">[RAF Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">rafmuseum.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">modern flying training 1970 present</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">During the 1970s the low-level tactical strike role of the RAF was developed.... In 1977 its principal aircraft were Hawker Hunters, including fighter, ground-attack and two-seat training versions. An Imperial War Museums film from the period shows a Brawdy instructor explaining the weapons fitted to a Hunter FGA.9, confirming that the station’s work went beyond ordinary circuit-and-landing practice. The first Hawk-trained students did not arrive at Brawdy until November 1977, and the Hawk replaced the Hunter gradually from 1978. Reports from the early and middle parts of the UFO wave should therefore be compared primarily with Hunter and supporting-aircraft activity, not routinely attributed to Hawks.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://film.iwmcollections.org.uk/record/33096" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: film.iwmcollections.org.uk">[iwmcollections.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">film.iwmcollections.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">IWM FilmSERVICING THE HUNTER FGA 9 - LINE CONDUCT &#x27;LIVE WEAPONS&#x27; [Main Title] &#124; IWM Film * Summary: RAF film in which a member of No.1 Ta...</span></span></span>

The station also hosted a search-and-rescue flight equipped with Westland Whirlwind helicopters. Taken together, these roles meant that local residents could encounter several kinds of military flying: fast jets crossing the coast, aircraft turning or climbing during exercises, helicopters moving more slowly at low level, and occasional visiting aircraft. This does not mean that every unusual light belonged to Brawdy, but it creates a far richer field of possible misidentifications than would exist around an ordinary rural village.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cawdor_Barracks" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Cawdor Barracks</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Wikipedia Cawdor Barracks Coordinates: 51°53′01′′N 005°07′26′′W Cawdor Barracks Near Brawdy, Pembrokeshire in...</span></span></span>

### The adjoining American facility

Next to the RAF station was Naval Facility Brawdy, commissioned in 1974 and jointly staffed by British and United States personnel. It formed part of the Sound Surveillance System, usually shortened to SOSUS, which used underwater acoustic arrays to detect and track submarines in the Atlantic. US Navy accounts describe SOSUS as an undersea-warfare system based on long-range detection of low-frequency sound.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RAF_Brawdy" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[wikipedia.org]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Bride&#x27;s Bay and seven miles east south east of St David&#x27;s Head. The secrecy surrounding that installation has encouraged speculation that Brawdy possessed hidden knowledge of the UFO reports. It is important, however, to distinguish submarine surveillance from aerial surveillance. SOSUS hydrophones listened underwater; they were not an air-defence radar network designed to track unidentified craft above Broad Haven. The facility’s existence establishes an important Cold War and American military presence, but it does not by itself imply that US personnel recorded the sightings.

Later proposals for an American over-the-horizon radar in Pembrokeshire are sometimes folded into the 1977 story. Parliamentary records show that such a radar trial was discussed in 1990, with a transmitter proposed for the former RAF St Davids airfield and associated equipment at Brawdy. That proposal came thirteen years after the main sighting wave and cannot explain events in 1977.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://hansard.parliament.uk/Commons/1990-04-23/debates/ca0daae2-10e6-4c78-80c9-b9f90a7080fb/Over-The-HorizonRadar" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: hansard.parliament.uk">[Hansard]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">hansard.parliament.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Hansard Over-The-Horizon Radar</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Hansard Over-The-Horizon Radar</span></span></span>

## How aircraft can become strange objects

Military flying is especially relevant to reports in which witnesses had only a brief view, lacked a known reference distance or saw an object against cloud, haze or a bright sky. A fast jet seen nearly head-on can appear almost stationary before changing position suddenly as it turns. Seen from the side, a swept-wing aircraft may look cigar-shaped; during a bank, its wings or underside can briefly produce a disc-like outline. Landing lights, navigation lights and reflected sunlight can further conceal the airframe.

Pembrokeshire’s coast makes such errors easier. Open water and large fields provide few reliable size cues, while headlands, rising ground and broken cloud can hide an aircraft and then reveal it again abruptly. Sound is also an unreliable guide. Wind direction, terrain and the difference between the speed of light and sound can make a jet seem silent at first, or make its noise appear to come from somewhere behind the visible object.

These effects could plausibly explain parts of the [1977 wave]({{ '1977-wave/' | relative_url }}), particularly fleeting lights and distant aerial shapes. They are less satisfactory for reports involving an apparently grounded object observed for an extended period, close-range heat effects or figures moving nearby. Those claims require separate examination because adding “military” to an explanation does not automatically reproduce every detail described by a witness.

The time of day matters as well. RAF training activity is a stronger candidate for a report made during normal flying hours than for an alleged encounter in the middle of the night, unless records show night flying or an emergency operation. A persuasive identification would require the date, time, viewing direction and reported movement to match an actual sortie. No publicly available flight log has yet supplied that level of confirmation for the central Broad Haven event.

## Could Harriers have caused the reports?

One recurring story attributes the strange craft to Harrier jump jets and the silver-clad figures to American service personnel. The suggestion has intuitive appeal because Harriers could hover or make short take-offs, behaviour that might have seemed extraordinary to an observer expecting a conventional aeroplane.

There are significant problems with treating this as an established solution. The Harrier was not a secret new aircraft in 1977: it had entered RAF squadron service in 1969. More importantly, RAF Brawdy’s documented training fleet during the main sighting period centred on Hawker Hunters, not a resident Harrier unit. Harriers could have visited or flown through the region, but a general possibility is not the same as evidence that one was present at the correct place and time.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.rafmuseum.org.uk/blog/harriers-over-falklands/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: rafmuseum.org.uk">[rafmuseum.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">rafmuseum.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">RAF Museum Harriers over Falklands</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">RAF Museum Harriers over Falklands</span></span></span>

The aircraft descriptions also do not map neatly onto a Harrier. Broad Haven witnesses commonly recalled a silvery or greenish object with a dome, while other reports described saucer-like forms. A Harrier seen at an unfamiliar angle might be misperceived, but explaining a particular sighting requires more than noting that vertical-take-off aircraft existed.

The Harrier theory is therefore best regarded as a testable hypothesis that remains undocumented. It would become substantially stronger if a squadron record, logbook, exercise schedule or named crew placed a Harrier near the reported location at the relevant time. In the absence of such evidence, it should not be repeated as though the case has been solved.

## Were the “silver figures” military personnel?

Military and industrial protective clothing offers a more plausible comparison for some of the humanoid reports. After Rosa Granville reported an object and faceless figures near the Haven Fort Hotel at Little Haven, Flight Lieutenant Cowan of RAF Brawdy inspected the location. According to later accounts of his report, he found no physical evidence of a landing and observed that the described clothing resembled protective suits associated with firefighting or the nearby oil industry. He also considered the possibility that a practical joker was active.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://herald.wales/west-wales/pembrokeshire/the-welsh-triangle-what-was-seen-in-the-sky-back-in-1977/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: herald.wales">[Herald.Wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">herald.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-title">the welsh triangle what was seen in the sky back in 1977</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">the welsh triangle what was seen in the sky back in 1977</span></span></span>

That interpretation has several strengths. Fire-resistant suits, aluminised heat-protection clothing, helmets and breathing equipment can obscure the human face and produce an unfamiliar silhouette, particularly in poor light. Pembrokeshire also contained both military facilities and major oil installations around Milford Haven, so such equipment was not foreign to the area.

However, resemblance does not establish identity. No named serviceman has been tied by contemporary documentation to Granville’s encounter, and no official record has demonstrated that a Brawdy exercise took personnel in protective clothing to the hotel grounds. A later claim that an American serviceman was involved remains a retrospective account rather than a verified entry in an operations log.

There is also evidence that deliberate hoaxing occurred within the wider UFO atmosphere. Local businessman Glyn Edwards later admitted wearing a silver suit as a prank, although his admission cannot automatically explain sightings that happened at different places or times. The safest conclusion is that costumed people may have contributed to the wave, while the extent of their involvement remains uncertain.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://herald.wales/west-wales/pembrokeshire/the-welsh-triangle-what-was-seen-in-the-sky-back-in-1977/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: herald.wales">[Herald.Wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">herald.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-title">the welsh triangle what was seen in the sky back in 1977</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">the welsh triangle what was seen in the sky back in 1977</span></span></span>

## What did the official inquiry establish?

The Ministry of Defence did not conduct a full-scale scientific investigation of every Pembrokeshire report. Nevertheless, the surviving record shows more official interest than a simple dismissal. The National Archives holds a Ministry of Defence file covering UFO reports and correspondence from April to October 1977, the period in which the Pembrokeshire wave attracted political and press attention.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://discovery.nationalarchives.gov.uk/details/record?catid=8445287&amp;catln=6" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: discovery.nationalarchives.gov.uk">[Discovery]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">discovery.nationalarchives.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFO reports and correspondence: April-October 1977 &#124; The National ArchivesUFO reports and correspondence: April-October 1977 &#124; The Nation...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 1977</span></span></span>

Contemporary correspondence cited in later reporting indicates that officials considered whether a discreet inquiry by the RAF Provost and Security Service was warranted because some witnesses were regarded as level-headed. This was a security-oriented question: officials needed to decide whether anything represented an intrusion, defence concern, hoax or ordinary activity. It was not an endorsement of an extraterrestrial interpretation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://herald.wales/west-wales/pembrokeshire/the-welsh-triangle-what-was-seen-in-the-sky-back-in-1977/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: herald.wales">[Herald.Wales]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">herald.wales</span><span class="citation-popover-title">the welsh triangle what was seen in the sky back in 1977</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">the welsh triangle what was seen in the sky back in 1977</span></span></span>

What is missing is equally important. There is no publicly established radar plot, matched flight record, military photograph or formal technical identification connecting one of Brawdy’s aircraft to the school sighting. Nor has a released American record shown that Naval Facility Brawdy tracked an anomalous object. Official attention confirms that reports were taken seriously enough to check; it does not confirm what witnesses saw.

## What the military connection proves — and what it does not

RAF Brawdy changes the balance of probabilities. In an area used for tactical flying, an unfamiliar aircraft is a more economical first explanation than an unknown craft from elsewhere. The base also supplies plausible mechanisms for several recurring features of the reports:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--caution" markdown="1">

* **Odd movement:** jets turning towards or away from an observer can appear to hover, stop or accelerate abruptly.
* **Uncertain shape:** brief views, banking aircraft and strong lights can hide wings and distort outlines.
* **Unexpected noise:** wind, distance and delayed sound can make aircraft seem silent or place the noise in the wrong direction.
* **Strange figures:** military, firefighting or industrial protective clothing can resemble featureless silver suits.
* **Official caution:** a nearby RAF station and American undersea-surveillance facility gave defence officials legitimate reasons to check whether reports involved aircraft, trespass or security concerns.

</div>

These points make military activity a credible partial explanation. They also weaken arguments that Brawdy’s mere presence proves secret experimental craft or an organised cover-up. No reliable evidence has emerged that the station operated disc-shaped vehicles, and the neighbouring American installation’s documented purpose was submarine detection rather than the tracking of spacecraft.

The best overall assessment is therefore mixed. RAF flying, misjudged distance and service-related activity could account for an unknown share of the Pembrokeshire reports and should be tested before more exotic interpretations. Hoaxing may explain some figure encounters. But without sortie records matched to individual observations, military activity remains a strong contextual explanation rather than a single demonstrated solution to the entire 1977 wave.
