## Introduction

Llandrindod Wells has produced two of the clearest entries in [Radnorshire]({{ 'what-really-flew-over-radnorshires-dark/' | relative_url }})’s otherwise thin UFO record: an official Ministry of Defence report from 2003 and a privately preserved orange-red orb account dated 2012. Both describe conspicuous red or orange lights that appeared to hover or move slowly, but neither supplies the measurements, photographs, independent witnesses or investigative follow-up needed for a firm identification.



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Radnorshire_2d75e1_llandrindod_sighting_d92524-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Llandrindod" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

The 2003 entry matters because it is an authentic government record, not because the Ministry of Defence confirmed an extraordinary aircraft. The 2012 story is more descriptive, yet its surviving publication contains inconsistencies that weaken its value. Taken together, the reports establish that unusual lights were seen over Llandrindod Wells; they do not establish what produced them. Plausible possibilities range from balloons or lantern-like objects to aircraft viewed under misleading conditions, but the missing directional, weather and timing data prevent any explanation from being demonstrated.

## The 2003 Ministry of Defence report

At 9.35 pm on 14 June 2003, somebody reported an unusual display over Llandrindod Wells, then recorded under the modern administrative county of Powys. The Ministry of Defence’s published sightings table summarised it in one sentence: “Very big red light balls hovering. One red light hung smaller and then dropped out.”<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/5a75c656e5274a545822e1ea/UFOReports2003WholeoftheUK.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: assets.publishing.service.gov.uk">[GOV.UK]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">assets.publishing.service.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Reports 2002-2005 - Whole of the UK...</span></span></span>

That brief line is the strongest surviving evidence for the incident. It fixes a date, time and town, records that more than one red light was apparently present, and preserves the witness’s impression that the lights were hovering. It also suggests a change during the observation: one light either became smaller, appeared lower, faded or disappeared. The wording “dropped out” is too ambiguous to distinguish between physical descent, loss of brightness and simple obscuration.

The official source should not be mistaken for an official finding. The Ministry’s published tables were essentially registers of reports received, listing locations and short descriptions. The Llandrindod entry gives no witness occupation, unlike some other cases in the same document where a pilot, police officer or RAF connection was recorded. It contains no viewing direction, duration, elevation, estimated distance, apparent movement against landmarks, sound, weather, photographs or radar information.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/ufo-reports-in-the-uk" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: GOV.UK">[GOV.UK]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">GOV.UK</span><span class="citation-popover-title">From: Ministry of Defence</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFO reports in the UK - GOV.UKDecember 4, 2007 — UFO reports in the UK - GOV.UK Home Defence and armed forces FOI release UFO reports in...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 4, 2007</span></span></span>

This distinction is central to assessing the case. The document proves that a report entered the government system; it does not show that officials verified the account or eliminated ordinary explanations. The fuller 2003 correspondence file associated with Ministry UFO reporting remains closed because of personal-information restrictions, with a scheduled opening date of 2088. It is therefore possible that protected correspondence once contained further witness details, but the accessible catalogue does not demonstrate that the Llandrindod report received a technical investigation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://discovery.nationalarchives.gov.uk/details/r/C11691605" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: discovery.nationalarchives.gov.uk">[Discovery National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">discovery.nationalarchives.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Discovery National ArchivesUFO correspondence &#124; The National ArchivesUFO correspondence &#124; The National Archives Home Discovery DEFE 24/20...</span></span></span>

### What might “hovering” have meant?

A light can appear stationary without being physically suspended over the landscape. An aircraft approaching almost directly towards an observer may show little sideways movement. A distant balloon or lantern can drift slowly enough to look fixed, particularly when there are few visible reference points. A light may also seem to fall when it dims, passes behind cloud or terrain, or changes direction away from the observer.

The description of “very big” lights is similarly difficult to convert into a real size. Witnesses generally estimate the angular size of an unexplained light—the amount of the sky it seems to cover—not its physical diameter. Without a reliable distance, a small nearby object and a powerful distant light can produce comparable impressions.

Astronomy offers no simple complete answer. The Moon was full on 14 June 2003 and would have been a prominent object around the evening, but a single full Moon does not naturally account for several red light balls or one apparently dropping out. It could nevertheless have affected visual contrast and the perception of nearby cloud or lights. No surviving direction is given, so even a basic comparison between the sighting and the Moon’s position cannot be made securely.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://theskylive.com/moon/2003" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: theskylive.com">[The Sky Live]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">theskylive.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Without your location, we will use Greenwhich as a default, b</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Sky LiveMoon Phase on June 14, 2003: Full Moon &#124; TheSkyLiveJune 14, 2003 — Moon Phase on June 14, 2003: Full Moon &#124; TheSkyLive Please...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 14, 2003</span></span></span>

Lanterns are another reasonable candidate, though not a proven solution. Flame-powered sky lanterns characteristically appear orange or red, move silently with the wind and may seem to hover before fading or descending. They later became a frequent explanation for British mass-[light reports]({{ 'light-reports/' | relative_url }}), especially during the sharp rise in reports received by the Ministry near the end of the decade. The 2003 Llandrindod case predates that best-documented surge, however, and the available entry contains no wind, duration or flight-path information with which to test the theory.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cdn.nationalarchives.gov.uk/documents/final-tranche-of-UFO-files-released.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cdn.nationalarchives.gov.uk">[cdn.nationalarchives.gov.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cdn.nationalarchives.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Source details in endnotes.</span></span></span>

## The 2012 orange-red orb account

A later report describes a bright orange-red orb over Llandrindod Wells at 8.35 pm on 12 February 2012. According to the version preserved by Swansea UFO Network, a witness identified as Linda noticed the light after leaving her house to use an outbuilding. She estimated that it was about 200 feet above the ground and nearly a metre across. It reportedly moved slowly above a tree line, continued towards houses several streets away, made no sound and then disappeared in a flash near distant trees.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sufon.co.uk/return-of-the-triangles" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sufon.co.uk">[Swansea Ufo Network]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sufon.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">return of the triangles</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">return of the triangles</span></span></span>

This account contains more observational texture than the Ministry entry. It includes a named—but not fully identified—witness, a clear night, nearby visual reference points, apparent movement across the scene and an explicit statement that no sound was heard. The witness also rejected a Chinese-lantern explanation, saying that the light was low enough and sufficiently orb-like for her to distinguish it from one.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sufon.co.uk/return-of-the-triangles" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sufon.co.uk">[Swansea Ufo Network]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sufon.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">return of the triangles</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">return of the triangles</span></span></span>

Those details make the report interesting, but not necessarily more reliable. Estimates of an object’s height and diameter cannot be independently calculated without its actual distance. The quoted figures of 200 feet and one metre therefore appear to be impressions rather than measured values. The reference to a tree line and houses could have helped reconstruct the sighting, but no street, bearing, map, photograph or precise route is supplied.

The reported silence also has limited identifying power. A genuinely nearby powered aircraft would usually be expected to produce sound, but a distant aircraft, balloon, lantern or other windborne light might be inaudible. The slow movement followed by abrupt disappearance could indicate extinction of a flame, passage behind trees, cloud or terrain, or a change in viewing angle. It does not by itself demonstrate sudden acceleration or disappearance in open sky.

### Problems in the surviving record

The largest weakness is the way the account has been transmitted. Swansea UFO Network’s page places the story beneath a heading for a “black triangle” database, although the witness narrative describes an orange-red orb and gives no triangular structure. Its database index likewise labels the 12 February 2012 Llandrindod Wells case as a black triangle.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sufon.co.uk/return-of-the-triangles" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sufon.co.uk">[Swansea Ufo Network]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sufon.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">return of the triangles</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">return of the triangles</span></span></span>

The page also attributes the account to the former UK-UFO website but displays a reporting date of “29 September 2010”, which is earlier than the stated 2012 event. Elsewhere on the same page, material from separate reports appears close together, making a copying or formatting error more likely than evidence that the event date itself was verified incorrectly.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sufon.co.uk/return-of-the-triangles" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sufon.co.uk">[Swansea Ufo Network]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sufon.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">return of the triangles</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">return of the triangles</span></span></span>

These inconsistencies do not prove that the witness invented the sighting. They show that the surviving version is a secondary database transcription whose chain of custody is unclear. The original submission, its metadata and any correspondence with the witness are not presented. Consequently, readers cannot determine whether “black triangle”, the 2010 reporting date or other details arose in the original report or were introduced during later compilation.

The witness’s rejection of a lantern deserves to be recorded, but it is not conclusive. Witnesses can correctly recognise familiar objects, yet judging the shape of a bright point at night is difficult. Glare, poor distance perception and atmospheric softness can turn a small flame or light source into a rounded orb. Conversely, it would also be unfair to declare it a lantern merely because its colour and slow, silent motion resemble one. The evidence supports lantern-like behaviour, not a verified lantern identification.

## What the surviving evidence can prove

The two Llandrindod Wells reports share a recognisable pattern: red or orange luminous objects, apparent hovering or slow movement, little or no information about physical structure, and disappearance without recovery of material evidence. That resemblance is noteworthy, but a pair of reports separated by nearly nine years is not enough to demonstrate a recurring phenomenon or local “flap”.

The evidence can support several limited conclusions:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--metric" markdown="1">

* **A report was officially logged in 2003.** The government table securely establishes the date, time, location and short description received by the Ministry of Defence.
* **A more detailed private account was circulated for 2012.** It describes an orange-red, silent light moving over trees and houses, but survives through a later UFO database rather than an official record.
* **Neither case contains objective corroboration.** No photograph, video, radar trace, identifiable second witness, aviation record or physical evidence has been located in the published material.
* **Neither case has been conclusively explained.** Ordinary possibilities remain viable, but the data required to select among them were not preserved.
* **“Unidentified” here means insufficiently documented.** It does not amount to evidence that an unknown craft, still less an extraterrestrial vehicle, was present.

</div>

The 2003 report is therefore stronger in provenance but extremely weak in detail. The 2012 account is richer in narrative detail but weaker in provenance and internally untidy in its published form. Combining them does not repair either problem: two incomplete descriptions cannot supply the missing measurements or independent verification.

This is consistent with the wider purpose and limitations of the former Ministry UFO system. The Ministry recorded reports principally to consider possible defence implications, not to certify unexplained objects as extraordinary. When the UFO desk closed in 2009, officials said that decades of reports had produced no indication of an extraterrestrial presence or a military threat and that continuing the service no longer served a defence purpose.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cdn.nationalarchives.gov.uk/documents/final-tranche-of-UFO-files-released.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cdn.nationalarchives.gov.uk">[cdn.nationalarchives.gov.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cdn.nationalarchives.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Source details in endnotes.</span></span></span>

## Why Llandrindod Wells still matters

Within historic Radnorshire, Llandrindod Wells remains important because it offers the county’s clearest pairing of an official report and a later civilian account. It shows both the value and the limitations of UFO archives. A government entry can preserve an otherwise forgotten event, while still omitting nearly everything needed to investigate it. A private database can retain the witness’s fuller story, while introducing uncertainty about dates, categories and transcription.

The fairest classification for both sightings is **unresolved but weakly evidenced**. They are not debunked, because no explanation has been proved. They are also not strong unknowns, because the surviving information never reaches the standard needed to exclude aircraft, balloons, lantern-like lights, astronomical misperception or obscuration by the local landscape.

Later reporting has not materially strengthened either claim. No corroborating photographs, additional witnesses or official analytical findings have emerged in the accessible record. Instead, the documentary problems surrounding the 2012 entry slightly weaken confidence in its exact wording and categorisation. What remains is a modest but useful case family: two sincere-sounding reports of striking lights over Llandrindod Wells, preserved well enough to document local UFO history but not well enough to determine what was actually in the sky.
