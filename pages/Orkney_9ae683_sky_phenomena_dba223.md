## Introduction

[Orkney]({{ 'what-really-happened-in-orkney-skies/' | relative_url }})’s position at the northern edge of the UK makes its skies especially capable of producing reports of unusual lights that are later understood as natural events. The same dark, open horizons that make the islands attractive for astronomy also remove many everyday reference points, making distant or unfamiliar objects harder to judge. Aurora displays, meteors, bright planets and other atmospheric or space-related phenomena can therefore appear mysterious when first seen.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.orkney.com/things/nature/northern-lights" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: orkney.com">[Orkney.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">orkney.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Northern Lights &#124; Orkney.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Northern Lights &#124; Orkney.com</span></span></span>

## Aurora and meteor sightings

Aurora is one of the most important natural mechanisms behind unusual-looking skies in Orkney. The islands are among the better locations in the UK for seeing the Northern Lights because of their northerly latitude, low light pollution and wide views across the horizon. Locally known as the “Merry Dancers”, aurora displays can appear as moving curtains, glowing arcs or shifting patches of colour.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.orkney.com/things/nature/northern-lights" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: orkney.com">[Orkney.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">orkney.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Northern Lights &#124; Orkney.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Northern Lights &#124; Orkney.com</span></span></span>

The science behind the display also explains why it can be mistaken for something artificial. Auroras occur when charged particles from the Sun interact with Earth’s magnetic field and collide with gases high in the atmosphere. These collisions release light, with oxygen and nitrogen producing different colours depending on altitude and conditions. Green is the most common colour, while red, pink and purple tones can also appear during stronger displays.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://science.nasa.gov/sun/auroras/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: science.nasa.gov">[NASA Science]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">science.nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Science Auroras</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NASA ScienceAuroras - NASA ScienceFebruary 4, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 4, 2025</span></span></span>

To an observer without astronomical context, an aurora can share several features often mentioned in unusual sky reports:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Movement:** the lights can ripple, pulse or appear to travel across the sky.
* **Changing shape:** curtains and bands can form, disappear and reform within minutes.
* **Unusual colours:** green or reddish glows may seem unlike ordinary lighting.
* **Large apparent size:** because auroras occur high in the atmosphere, they can appear to cover much of the sky.

</div>

These characteristics make aurora a particularly relevant explanation for reports from northern Scotland and island communities, where observers may see dramatic displays without nearby urban lighting to provide scale.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.orkney.com/things/nature/northern-lights" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: orkney.com">[Orkney.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">orkney.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Northern Lights &#124; Orkney.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Northern Lights &#124; Orkney.com</span></span></span>

Meteors provide a different type of mistaken identification. Unlike aurora, which can last for minutes or hours, meteors usually create brief flashes or streaks lasting only seconds. A bright meteor, known as a fireball, can appear as a glowing object moving rapidly across the sky, sometimes breaking apart or leaving a trail. Such events are routinely reported as unusual because their speed and brightness do not match normal aircraft behaviour.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://apnews.com/article/42f53f511af6b9e9deafc34633adac99" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: apnews.com">[AP News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">apnews.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">AP News How common are fireballs streaking across the sky?</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Most go unnoticed as they appear over oceans, unpopulated areas, or during daylight. Scottish skies have produced notable fireball reports visible over wide areas. A 2016 Scottish fireball, for example, generated widespread public interest before being identified as a meteor event rather than an unexplained object. ## Mistaking natural events for objects

The difficulty in identifying sky phenomena is not simply a lack of knowledge. Human observation is poorly suited to judging objects in an empty night sky, especially when there are no familiar reference points. A bright light over the sea, for example, may be impossible to judge accurately because an observer cannot easily estimate whether it is hundreds of metres away, several kilometres away, or much higher in the atmosphere.

Several natural factors increase the chance of confusion in Orkney:

**Distance and scale are difficult to judge.**

A meteor several kilometres above the ground can appear nearby, while an auroral feature hundreds of kilometres away may appear directly overhead. Without landmarks or measured data, witnesses may unintentionally misjudge speed and size.

**Dark skies make rare events stand out.**

Orkney’s limited light pollution is an advantage for astronomy but also means that unusual lights receive more attention. A bright planet, satellite or meteor can appear much more striking against a dark background than it would from a city.

**Weather and atmosphere alter appearances.**

Cloud layers, haze and reflections can distort the shape and brightness of lights. The National Archives’ collection of UK UFO records shows that official sighting reports often included weather conditions because visibility and atmospheric conditions could affect interpretation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/explore-the-collection/explore-by-time-period/postwar/ufo-reports/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationalarchives.gov.uk">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationalarchives.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives UFO reports</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesUFO reports - The National Archives...</span></span></span>

**Memory changes after a surprising event.**

A dramatic sight may be remembered as longer, brighter or more unusual than it was. This does not mean witnesses are unreliable; rather, it reflects the difficulty of estimating unfamiliar events without instruments or recordings.

For UFO investigations, the key question is therefore not whether a witness saw something, but whether there is enough supporting evidence to identify what was seen. Photographs with reliable timestamps, multiple independent observations, radar information or aviation records can transform an unusual report into a clearer investigation. Without those details, many cases remain simply “unidentified” rather than evidence of an extraordinary cause.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/help-with-your-research/research-guides/ufos/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationalarchives.gov.uk">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationalarchives.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives UFOs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesUFOs - The National Archives...</span></span></span>

## Why dark skies affect perception

Orkney’s skies create a particular combination of opportunity and uncertainty. The islands are excellent places to observe genuine astronomical and atmospheric events, but the same conditions can make identification harder for casual observers.

A city observer often has clues: aircraft routes, buildings, street lighting and familiar urban references. An Orkney observer may instead have an uninterrupted horizon over land or sea. This creates a stronger visual experience but fewer ways to estimate distance or movement.

The result is that ordinary phenomena can acquire an unusual character. A meteor crossing a clear winter sky may appear like a controlled object because it moves quickly and brightly. An aurora may seem like a structured display because its curtains and waves change shape. A bright planet near the horizon may appear to move or flicker because of atmospheric distortion.

This pattern fits the wider UK record of UFO reporting. The official archive contains many examples where the initial description was unusual but later assessment pointed towards familiar causes such as planets, aircraft, satellites or atmospheric effects. The important distinction is that “unidentified” describes the state of the evidence at the time, not a conclusion about what the object was.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationalarchives.gov.uk/explore-the-collection/explore-by-time-period/postwar/ufo-reports/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationalarchives.gov.uk">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationalarchives.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives UFO reports</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesUFO reports - The National Archives...</span></span></span>

For Orkney, natural sky phenomena are therefore not a dismissal of unusual reports but a necessary part of understanding them. The islands’ spectacular skies genuinely produce memorable events; the challenge is separating rare but natural sights from cases where evidence suggests something genuinely unexplained.
