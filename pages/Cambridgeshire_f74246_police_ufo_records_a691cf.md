## Introduction

[Cambridgeshire]({{ 'what-really-happened-in/' | relative_url }}) Constabulary’s records show that 47 incidents described or retrieved as UFO reports were logged during 2024. Officers were recorded as attending 24, while 23 were not attended. That is evidence that unusual aerial reports entered the police control system and sometimes required a practical response. It is not evidence that officers confirmed 47 extraordinary aircraft, still less extraterrestrial craft.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambs.police.uk/foi-ai/cambridgeshire-police/foi/2025/april/reports-of-ufos/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambs.police.uk">[Cambridgeshire Constabulary]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambs.police.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Cambridgeshire Constabulary Reports of UFOs &#124; Cambridgeshire Constabulary</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridgeshire ConstabularyReports of UFOs &#124; Cambridgeshire ConstabularyApril 16, 2025 — Reports of UFOs &#124; Cambridgeshire Constabulary Re...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 16, 2025</span></span></span>



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Cambridgeshire_f74246_police_ufo_records_a691cf-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Police Records" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

The most revealing point is what police data are designed to measure. An incident log records information received, the possible risks identified and the action taken. It does not normally establish what an object ultimately was. A caller reporting unexplained lights may be describing a drone, aircraft, balloon, lantern, astronomical object or something that remains unidentified because too little information was collected. The 2024 figures therefore tell us more about public reporting and police risk assessment than about the underlying nature of Cambridgeshire’s UFO sightings.

## How the 47 incidents were counted

The figure comes from a Freedom of Information response published by Cambridgeshire Constabulary on 16 April 2025. The request asked how many UFO reports the force received between 1 January and 31 December 2024, what action was taken and what the median response time was. The published answer gave a total of 47 reports, divided into 24 attended and 23 not attended. It did not provide dates, locations, witness descriptions, classifications or final explanations for the individual incidents.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambs.police.uk/foi-ai/cambridgeshire-police/foi/2025/april/reports-of-ufos/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambs.police.uk">[Cambridgeshire Constabulary]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambs.police.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Cambridgeshire Constabulary Reports of UFOs &#124; Cambridgeshire Constabulary</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridgeshire ConstabularyReports of UFOs &#124; Cambridgeshire ConstabularyApril 16, 2025 — Reports of UFOs &#124; Cambridgeshire Constabulary Re...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 16, 2025</span></span></span>

That missing detail is crucial. The disclosure does not say that every caller used “UFO” in the popular sense of a mysterious structured craft. It does not reveal whether entries were found through a dedicated incident category, a keyword search of free-text comments or another extraction method. Nor does it show whether several calls related to the same event. The total should therefore be read as the number of matching police records returned by the force’s search, not necessarily 47 separate objects or sighting episodes.

The constabulary itself described the figures as an “un-audited snapshot” drawn from live, unpublished systems and subject to the interpretation of the person extracting them. That warning is not a minor formality. It means the total may depend on spelling, terminology, database fields and the wording of the original request.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambs.police.uk/foi-ai/cambridgeshire-police/foi/2025/april/reports-of-ufos/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambs.police.uk">[Cambridgeshire Constabulary]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambs.police.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Cambridgeshire Constabulary Reports of UFOs &#124; Cambridgeshire Constabulary</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridgeshire ConstabularyReports of UFOs &#124; Cambridgeshire ConstabularyApril 16, 2025 — Reports of UFOs &#124; Cambridgeshire Constabulary Re...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 16, 2025</span></span></span>

A second Cambridgeshire disclosure demonstrates the problem more clearly. An earlier request searched incidents from August 2019 to July 2024 for a broad group of terms including UFOs, aliens, ghosts and other paranormal language. It returned annual totals ranging from 27 to 51 and classified most records as public-safety incidents. However, those figures mixed several keywords and therefore cannot be treated as a clean historical UFO series.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambs.police.uk/foi-ai/cambridgeshire-police/foi/2024/september/ghosts-ufos-and-paranormal-activity/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambs.police.uk">[Cambridgeshire Constabulary]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambs.police.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridgeshire ConstabularyGhosts, UFOs and Paranormal Activity &#124; Cambridgeshire ConstabularySeptember 20, 2024 — Ghosts, UFOs and Parano...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 20, 2024</span></span></span>

An even broader 2025 search produced 130 keyword matches for 2024, but the force warned that the data were of “very low quality”. It said many matches probably did not refer to paranormal claims at all: words could appear in comments, unrelated contexts or What3words location identifiers. Only two records across the five-year table contained a requested term in the incident description itself.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambs.police.uk/foi-ai/cambridgeshire-police/foi/2025/may/ghosts--demons-etc/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambs.police.uk">[Cambridgeshire Constabulary]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambs.police.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Cambridgeshire Constabulary Ghosts &amp; Demons etc &#124; Cambridgeshire Constabulary</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridgeshire ConstabularyGhosts &amp; Demons etc &#124; Cambridgeshire ConstabularyMay 19, 2025 — Ghosts &amp; Demons etc &#124; Cambridgeshire Constabul...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 19, 2025</span></span></span>

These overlapping disclosures are not necessarily contradictory. They appear to answer differently framed requests using different search fields and keyword sets. Together, they show why a headline count must not be separated from the method that produced it.

## Why officers attended some reports

A police visit does not mean that officers judged a report to be extraordinary. It means that the information available at the time justified deployment. Call handlers grade incidents according to seriousness and the speed of response required; Cambridgeshire Constabulary describes this as assessing each call before deciding how urgently officers or staff should act.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambs.police.uk/police-forces/cambridgeshire-constabulary/areas/campaigns/campaigns/inside-the-control-room/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambs.police.uk">[Cambridgeshire Constabulary]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambs.police.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on police.uk.</span></span></span>

An unfamiliar object in the sky can raise ordinary policing questions that cannot safely be dismissed over the telephone. The immediate concern might include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* a drone flying close to people, roads, prisons, airports or helicopter landing sites;
* an object apparently descending, burning or threatening to crash;
* lights that could indicate an aircraft in difficulty;
* suspected surveillance, nuisance or criminal use of a drone;
* a vulnerable or distressed caller whose welfare needs checking;
* debris, flares or other objects presenting a fire or public-safety hazard.

</div>

This is consistent with the broader Cambridgeshire keyword data. Of the 26 matching incidents recorded between January and July 2024 in the paranormal-terms disclosure, 20 were placed in the public-safety category, four under crime, one under anti-social behaviour and one under administration. Earlier years showed the same pattern: public safety consistently accounted for most of the records.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambs.police.uk/foi-ai/cambridgeshire-police/foi/2024/september/ghosts-ufos-and-paranormal-activity/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambs.police.uk">[Cambridgeshire Constabulary]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambs.police.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridgeshire ConstabularyGhosts, UFOs and Paranormal Activity &#124; Cambridgeshire ConstabularySeptember 20, 2024 — Ghosts, UFOs and Parano...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 20, 2024</span></span></span>

Drone reports are especially relevant. The Civil Aviation Authority directs members of the public to contact police about dangerous or illegal drone flying, including activity near airports, prisons, crowds or hospital helicopter sites, above the normal 120-metre limit, or in controlled or restricted airspace. Immediate dangers should be reported through 999. Police attendance in such circumstances is evidence of a possible aviation or security concern, not an endorsement of a witness’s interpretation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.caa.co.uk/drones/reporting-concerns-about-safety-privacy-and-illegal-flying/concerns-about-privacy-and-illegal-use-of-drones/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: caa.co.uk">[CAA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">caa.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on caa.co.uk.</span></span></span>

A narrowly framed Cambridgeshire request covering 22–27 November 2024 found 16 incidents containing “drone” or “drones”, but none containing “UFO”. That short period coincided with intense public and official concern about unidentified drone activity around East Anglian RAF bases, although the Cambridgeshire records cannot automatically be linked to those military-site events. The result nevertheless illustrates how similar observations may be logged as drones rather than UFOs when callers or operators consider a conventional unmanned aircraft more likely.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.whatdotheyknow.com/request/drone_reports_november_2024?locale=en" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: whatdotheyknow.com">[WhatDoTheyKnow]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">whatdotheyknow.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">What Do They Know Drone Reports</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">What Do They Know Drone Reports</span></span></span>

## What the response times do and do not show

The force published 23 response-time values, ranging from zero minutes to 4,892 minutes, despite stating that 24 incidents were attended. Calculating the median of the 23 listed values gives 30 minutes. However, the mismatch between 24 attended incidents and 23 supplied timings means that this should be treated as a calculation from the published list, not a confirmed force statistic.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambs.police.uk/foi-ai/cambridgeshire-police/foi/2025/april/reports-of-ufos/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambs.police.uk">[Cambridgeshire Constabulary]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambs.police.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Cambridgeshire Constabulary Reports of UFOs &#124; Cambridgeshire Constabulary</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridgeshire ConstabularyReports of UFOs &#124; Cambridgeshire ConstabularyApril 16, 2025 — Reports of UFOs &#124; Cambridgeshire Constabulary Re...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 16, 2025</span></span></span>

The wide range is more informative than the median alone. Several recorded responses occurred within about 12 to 40 minutes, suggesting that some reports were treated as relatively immediate matters. Others took many hours or more than two days. Such variation is expected when records cover incidents of differing urgency, quality and purpose.

A zero-minute value could reflect immediate officer availability, an officer already present, or a database timestamp convention. A response lasting thousands of minutes could represent a scheduled visit, delayed resolution or the way arrival times were recorded. Without the underlying logs, it is impossible to know. The figures should not be converted into a league table of how seriously police took each UFO claim.

Modern police call handling centres on threat, harm, risk, investigative opportunity and vulnerability. Deployment decisions therefore depend on the practical circumstances described by the caller, not on whether an aerial object is interesting as a UFO case. National policing guidance also distinguishes urgent responses, scheduled attendance and resolution without deployment.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://library.college.police.uk/docs/homeoffice/call_handling_standards.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: library.college.police.uk">[College Police Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">library.college.police.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on police.uk.</span></span></span>

## Limits of police data as UFO evidence

Police records are useful because they establish that a report was made and that the force took a recorded decision. They can sometimes preserve a time, approximate location, initial description and response that would otherwise disappear. In a well-documented case, those details can be compared with aircraft movements, astronomical conditions, weather, drone activity, photographs or reports from independent witnesses.

The published Cambridgeshire figures lack nearly all of those features. They provide no object descriptions, directions, duration, elevation, weather conditions, images, radar information or independent corroboration. They also contain no final classifications showing that an incident remained unexplained after investigation. The dataset therefore cannot distinguish a genuinely puzzling observation from a readily identifiable aircraft or a report too vague to assess.

This difference matters because a police log preserves a claim before it has necessarily been verified. Official records-management guidance requires forces to assess operational information for provenance, completeness, accuracy, duplication, risk and continuing relevance. The existence of a record is not itself a finding that every statement within it is accurate.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/government/uploads/system/uploads/attachment_data/file/1172598/Code-of-Practice-on-police-information-and-records-management-2023-print.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: assets.publishing.service.gov.uk">[GOV.UK Assets]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">assets.publishing.service.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on service.gov.uk.</span></span></span>

The word “unidentified” is also time-sensitive. An object may be unidentified to the caller at 10.15 pm, suspected to be a drone by a call handler at 10.20 pm and recognised as an aircraft when officers arrive. Unless the final result is disclosed, the incident can continue to appear in a UFO search even though the uncertainty was temporary.

Conversely, “no further action” would not prove that an object was explained. A sighting may remain unresolved simply because it posed no danger, had disappeared, lacked evidence or offered no realistic line of enquiry. Unresolved is therefore not the same as extraordinary; it may only mean that the record cannot support a reliable conclusion.

## What the logs really reveal about Cambridgeshire

The 2024 disclosure provides three defensible findings. First, unusual aerial reports are not merely part of folklore: people do contact Cambridgeshire police about them. Secondly, approximately half of the retrieved incidents led to recorded attendance, showing that some calls contained enough potential risk or uncertainty to justify deployment. Thirdly, the figures are too thin to establish a sighting cluster, hotspot or unexplained technological phenomenon.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambs.police.uk/foi-ai/cambridgeshire-police/foi/2025/april/reports-of-ufos/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambs.police.uk">[Cambridgeshire Constabulary]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambs.police.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Cambridgeshire Constabulary Reports of UFOs &#124; Cambridgeshire Constabulary</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridgeshire ConstabularyReports of UFOs &#124; Cambridgeshire ConstabularyApril 16, 2025 — Reports of UFOs &#124; Cambridgeshire Constabulary Re...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: April 16, 2025</span></span></span>

The wider keyword disclosures strengthen the governance interpretation. Reports containing UFO or paranormal language are usually absorbed into ordinary police categories such as public safety, crime and anti-social behaviour. They are not handled through a specialist UFO investigation unit. Their significance depends on what officers might need to prevent or clarify: unsafe flying, nuisance, danger, distress or suspicious conduct.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambs.police.uk/foi-ai/cambridgeshire-police/foi/2024/september/ghosts-ufos-and-paranormal-activity/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambs.police.uk">[Cambridgeshire Constabulary]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambs.police.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridgeshire ConstabularyGhosts, UFOs and Paranormal Activity &#124; Cambridgeshire ConstabularySeptember 20, 2024 — Ghosts, UFOs and Parano...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 20, 2024</span></span></span>

Geography also needs care. Cambridgeshire Constabulary covers the modern policing area, including Peterborough and Huntingdonshire as well as Cambridge, the Fens, East Cambridgeshire and South Cambridgeshire. The broader 2019–24 disclosure recorded the largest number of keyword matches in Peterborough, but those figures combined UFO language with many other terms and cannot support a claim that Peterborough was a UFO hotspot.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cambs.police.uk/foi-ai/cambridgeshire-police/foi/2024/september/ghosts-ufos-and-paranormal-activity/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cambs.police.uk">[Cambridgeshire Constabulary]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cambs.police.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Cambridgeshire ConstabularyGhosts, UFOs and Paranormal Activity &#124; Cambridgeshire ConstabularySeptember 20, 2024 — Ghosts, UFOs and Parano...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 20, 2024</span></span></span>

For Cambridgeshire’s wider UFO history, the police figures are best treated as an index pointing towards possible cases, not as the cases themselves. A stronger release would need anonymised incident narratives, precise search criteria, dates and broad locations, separate counts for duplicate calls, actions taken, and recorded outcomes. Only then could researchers test reports against aviation data, drone restrictions, astronomical objects and independent testimony.

As published, the 47 incidents prove that unusual-sky calls reached the constabulary and were assessed through normal policing procedures. They do not prove that officers encountered extraordinary craft. The clearest lesson is administrative rather than extraterrestrial: when the public cannot identify something overhead, police records first capture a possible problem, not a confirmed answer.
