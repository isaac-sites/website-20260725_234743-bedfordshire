## Introduction

Radar is the feature that makes the 21 October 1952 [Little Rissington]({{ '1952-encounter/' | relative_url }}) encounter stand out in [Gloucestershire]({{ 'what-really-happened-in-gloucestershires/' | relative_url }})’s UFO history. A contemporary Royal Air Force entry states that Gloucester Air Traffic Control Centre later reported radar plots that “confirmed” the sighting made by Flight Lieutenant Michael Swiney and Royal Navy Lieutenant David Crofts. That is meaningful corroboration: the radar claim was recorded at the time, rather than being added to the story decades later.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://drdavidclarke.co.uk/secret-files/operation-mainbrace-ufos/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: drdavidclarke.co.uk">[Dr. David Clarke]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">drdavidclarke.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">operation mainbrace ufos</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">David ClarkeOperation Mainbrace UFOs &#124;renewed concern, and the determination to prevent service personnel speaking about a phenomenon tha...</span></span></span>

It is not, however, the same as having the radar evidence itself. No surviving plot, controller’s log, equipment record or sequence of measured positions is publicly available. Accounts of fighters being scrambled and objects travelling at 600 knots, 1,000 mph or even 3,000 mph come mainly from later testimony and retellings, and sometimes disagree over direction and the number of targets. Radar therefore strengthens the case, but cannot now establish exactly what was tracked, whether it matched the pilots’ objects, or how fast it moved.

## What the surviving RAF entry actually says

The strongest radar evidence is a short entry in the Central Flying School operations record book for RAF Little Rissington. It records that Swiney and Crofts saw three mysterious “saucer shaped objects” travelling at high speed at about 35,000 feet during a Meteor VII navigation exercise. It then adds: “Later, A.T.C.C. Gloucester reported radar plots to confirm this,” before noting that the Air Ministry discounted an extraterrestrial explanation. The document is preserved under the National Archives reference AIR 29/2310.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.uapmedia.uk/articles/swineycrofts" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: uapmedia.uk">[UAP Media UK]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">uapmedia.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UAP Media UKThe Swiney/Crofts CASE OF 1952 — UAP Media UKFebruary 28, 2022 — The Swiney/Crofts CASE OF 1952 — UAP Media UK The Swiney/Cro...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 28, 2022</span></span></span>

This wording deserves careful treatment. It establishes three useful points:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--benefit" markdown="1">

* **The radar element was contemporary.** It was entered into an official unit record close to the event, not reconstructed solely from the pilots’ memories.
* **The report was attributed to Gloucester air traffic control.** This points towards a civil or military air-traffic surveillance source in the Staverton area, rather than proving that every later-mentioned RAF radar station tracked the same returns.
* **The entry says “plots”, but supplies no measurements.** It gives no times, bearings, ranges, altitudes, headings, signal strengths or number of radar contacts.

</div>

The word “confirm” is consequently stronger than the surviving data can support today. It may mean that unexplained radar returns appeared in broadly the same area and period as the pilots’ report. It does not demonstrate that controllers established a continuous track on three solid objects corresponding individually to the three shapes seen from the Meteor.

That distinction matters because radar corroboration is most persuasive when visual and instrument records can be compared point by point. Investigators would ideally want the Meteor’s known position, the time of each visual observation, successive coordinates for the unknown returns and evidence that several stations independently followed the same targets. None of that detailed material has entered the public record.

## Did RAF fighters attempt an interception?

The surviving Little Rissington operations entry does not mention interceptor aircraft. The scramble appears in later accounts based on what Crofts and Swiney said they were told after landing, together with recollections and historical reconstructions published many years later.

Crofts recalled that Air Ministry investigators told him the objects had been detected by radar, that fighters were alerted and scrambled, and that a target was moving eastwards at either 600 knots or 600 miles per hour. He said the interceptors made no visual contact and returned. Later reports associated the tracking with Sopley in Hampshire and communications routed through the RAF establishment at Rudloe Manor in Wiltshire.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://drdavidclarke.co.uk/secret-files/operation-mainbrace-ufos/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: drdavidclarke.co.uk">[Dr. David Clarke]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">drdavidclarke.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">operation mainbrace ufos</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">David ClarkeOperation Mainbrace UFOs &#124;renewed concern, and the determination to prevent service personnel speaking about a phenomenon tha...</span></span></span>

Another widely circulated reconstruction says two Meteor F.8 fighters were scrambled from RAF Tangmere in Sussex. In that version, controllers followed an unidentified return that accelerated or disappeared from the radar display at an estimated 1,000 mph, with the target described as moving towards the Atlantic rather than eastwards.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nicap.org/reports/521021gloucestershire_rep.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nicap.org">[Nicap]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nicap.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFO ReportUFO Report...</span></span></span>

These stories are not necessarily irreconcilable. Different radar stations could have observed different returns, and an initial eastward movement might have been followed by a change of course. Speeds could also have been calculated over different portions of a track. The difficulty is that no surviving operational documents demonstrate such a sequence. Without scramble records, controller logs, aircraft serials, sortie times or the pilots’ post-flight reports, attempts to combine the accounts into one precise interception narrative become speculative.

There is also an important evidential imbalance. The claim that fighters saw nothing is reasonably consistent across the later versions. The dramatic details — the exact base, target direction and calculated speed — are much less stable. The safest conclusion is that an interception may well have been attempted, but its operational history has not been independently reconstructed from contemporary records.

## Why the reported speeds conflict

The speed figures attached to the incident range from a comparatively plausible 600 knots to roughly 1,000 mph and, in some popular summaries, 3,000 mph. They should not be presented as interchangeable measurements.

**About 600 knots.** This figure comes from Crofts’s recollection of what an investigator told him. He was unsure whether the wording had been 600 knots or 600 miles per hour. That uncertainty alone produces a sizeable difference: 600 knots is about 690 mph. More importantly, Crofts was recalling a briefing rather than reading from a surviving radar calculation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://drdavidclarke.co.uk/secret-files/operation-mainbrace-ufos/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: drdavidclarke.co.uk">[Dr. David Clarke]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">drdavidclarke.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">operation mainbrace ufos</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">David ClarkeOperation Mainbrace UFOs &#124;renewed concern, and the determination to prevent service personnel speaking about a phenomenon tha...</span></span></span>

**About 1,000 mph.** This appears in a later reconstruction in which a radar return abruptly left the display or moved away while interception was attempted. No original plotting sheet is reproduced alongside the claim, so it is unclear whether the figure was calculated from several measured positions, estimated by a controller or introduced during later narration.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nicap.org/reports/521021gloucestershire_rep.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nicap.org">[Nicap]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nicap.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFO ReportUFO Report...</span></span></span>

**About 3,000 mph.** This figure is repeated on tertiary webpages, usually with a claim that three objects entered a southern RAF sector at that speed. It is not stated in the surviving Little Rissington record and is not supported by a publicly available sequence of radar plots. It should therefore be treated as a weakly sourced embellishment rather than a secure finding.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Little_Rissington_UFO_incident" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Little Rissington UFO incident</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">POSITION RAF Little Rissington is a ba...</span></span></span>

The contradictions are not a minor technical issue. A speed of approximately 600 knots would have been fast but not fantastically beyond the jet-aircraft performance of the period. A Meteor F.8 had a maximum speed in the region of 500 knots, depending on altitude and configuration, making interception difficult if a target already had a substantial lead.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.rafmuseum.org.uk/research/collections/gloster-meteor-f8/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: rafmuseum.org.uk">[RAF Museum]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">rafmuseum.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">RAF Museum Gloster Meteor F8</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">RAF MuseumGloster Meteor F8 - RAF Museum# Gloster Meteor F8... Museum: &#124; London...</span></span></span> A genuine 3,000 mph track, by contrast, would imply performance vastly beyond an early-1950s aircraft and would demand exceptionally strong documentation. The larger the claim, the more damaging the absence of the underlying measurements becomes.

The pilots themselves could not provide a dependable speed calculation. They did not know the objects’ size or distance, so apparent movement across the windscreen could not be translated reliably into miles per hour. Statements that the objects moved at “high speed” or vanished rapidly describe an impression, not a measured velocity.

## Could 1952 radar produce convincing false targets?

Radar was already an essential air-defence tool in 1952, but it was not an infallible camera. A radar display showed echoes produced by transmitted radio energy; operators had to decide whether each return represented an aircraft, weather, terrain, interference or another propagation effect.

One relevant problem is **anomalous propagation**, often shortened to “anaprop”. Under certain atmospheric conditions, especially temperature inversions, radar energy can bend more sharply than usual and strike the ground or distant structures. The resulting echoes may appear where no aircraft is present. Modern Met Office guidance still describes temperature inversions bending radar beams downwards and producing strong misleading returns.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.metoffice.gov.uk/binaries/content/assets/metofficegovuk/pdf/research/library-and-archive/library/publications/factsheets/factsheet_15-weather-radar-2020_2023.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: metoffice.gov.uk">[Met Office]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">metoffice.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Met Office Radar</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Meteorological Library and ArchiveSeptember 5, 2023 — Anomalous propagation (anaprop)... This was not an explanation invented long after the early UFO era. Radar specialists were actively studying unusual propagation and false targets during the early 1950s. Contemporary investigations of unidentified returns over Washington in 1952 found a close association between some anomalous targets and atmospheric inversions, although debate continued over whether propagation could account for every reported movement.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.cufon.org/cufon/wash_nat/wash_faa.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cufon.org">[Computer UFO Network]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cufon.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">wash faa</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">wash faa</span></span></span>

An anomalous echo can become especially confusing when an operator tries to track it as if it were an aircraft. Changes in signal strength, beam geometry or the reflecting region can make a return appear to jump, accelerate, split or disappear. A speed calculated from two uncertain positions may consequently describe movement of the radar echo rather than a physical object.

That does not prove that anomalous propagation caused the Gloucestershire plots. No weather profile, radar frequency, equipment description or original display record has been published for direct testing. Nor would a false radar target by itself explain why two pilots simultaneously reported three structured-looking objects. The point is narrower: the phrase “radar confirmed it” cannot exclude radar error unless the technical record shows that controllers obtained a stable, coherent track with behaviour characteristic of a real airborne target.

Other ordinary complications also remain possible. Controllers might have detected Swiney’s own Meteor, another aircraft in the area, separate returns that were retrospectively associated with the sighting, or a mixture of genuine traffic and clutter. The surviving sentence does not explain how Gloucester ATCC distinguished the supposed unknowns from known aircraft.

## What evidence would have made the radar claim decisive?

A firm reconstruction would require far more than the operations-book summary. The most valuable missing material would include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* timed radar plotting sheets showing successive ranges and bearings;
* records identifying the radar stations and equipment involved;
* the Meteor trainer’s route, altitude and radio transcript;
* weather observations capable of testing anomalous propagation;
* scramble and sortie records for any interceptors;
* statements from the radar controllers and interceptor pilots;
* Swiney’s and Crofts’s original written reports.

</div>

Such records could answer whether three returns were tracked, whether their positions matched the objects in the pilots’ line of sight and whether the speed estimates were measured or merely inferred. They could also reveal whether “Gloucester reported radar plots” meant continuous tracking or only one or two unexplained echoes.

The apparent loss or destruction of the detailed investigation file is therefore central to the case. Later researchers found the brief operations entry, but Swiney was reportedly unable to obtain his original statement, and surviving accounts indicate that many early Ministry of Defence UFO papers were not retained.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.uapmedia.uk/articles/swineycrofts_format-amp" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: uapmedia.uk">[UAP Media UK]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">uapmedia.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">swineycrofts format amp</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UAP Media UKThe Swiney/Crofts CASE OF 1952The Swiney/Crofts CASE OF 1952 UAP Media UK View Original The Swiney/Crofts CASE OF 1952 Februa...</span></span></span> Absence of a file does not imply suppression of extraordinary evidence; routine disposal policies, uneven record-keeping and the sensitivity of Cold War radar procedures are all possible reasons. It nevertheless prevents modern reviewers from checking the strongest claims.

## Did radar really confirm the Gloucestershire UFOs?

In the limited sense used by the contemporary RAF record, **yes**: Gloucester air traffic control reported radar plots regarded at the time as supporting the pilots’ sighting. That makes the Little Rissington encounter stronger than a case based solely on a later witness recollection.

In the stricter investigative sense, **confirmation remains unproven**. The surviving record does not show that radar tracked three objects corresponding to those seen by Swiney and Crofts, and it contains none of the technical data needed to validate the reported speeds. The fighter scramble is plausible but rests largely on later testimony, while accounts disagree over whether the target travelled east, towards the Atlantic, at roughly 600 knots, around 1,000 mph or at the much less credible figure of 3,000 mph.

The radar evidence therefore raises the Little Rissington case above most Gloucestershire UFO reports without turning it into proof of an extraordinary aircraft. Its lasting importance lies in the combination of trained witnesses, a contemporary official reference and apparent operational concern. Its lasting weakness is that the records capable of converting those elements into a testable reconstruction are missing.
