## Introduction

On 19 July 2013, the captain of an Airbus A320 flying at 34,000 feet over eastern [Berkshire]({{ 'what-really-happened-in-berkshires-ufo/' | relative_url }}) believed that a bright metallic object rushed directly towards the cockpit and passed only feet above the aircraft. The first officer did not see it, the aircraft’s collision-warning system showed no nearby traffic, and air traffic control found no corresponding radar return. Checks also eliminated the known aircraft and scheduled balloon releases in the area.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.airproxboard.org.uk/uploadedFiles/Content/Standard_content/Airprox_report_files/2013/Airprox%20Report%202013086.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: airproxboard.org.uk">[airproxboard.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">airproxboard.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Airprox Report 2013086</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Airprox Report 2013086</span></span></span>



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Berkshire_fa6d4a_heathrow_pilot_encou_f202e2-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Pilot Encounter" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

The investigation therefore ended without identifying a physical object or confirming that a genuine near-collision had occurred. The UK Airprox Board considered a fleeting reflection from another aircraft, combined with the captain’s head movement, but judged the evidence too sparse for a meaningful conclusion. That mixture of a credible aviation witness, formal safety investigation and unresolved perception makes this Berkshire’s strongest modern aviation-related UFO case—but not proof of an extraordinary craft.

## The captain’s account and flight circumstances

The encounter occurred at approximately 18:35 UTC, 19.5 nautical miles west of Heathrow, at coordinates 51°26′ north, 0°58′ west. This places the reported closest approach above eastern Berkshire, although the aircraft was not necessarily travelling to or from Heathrow. It was cruising northwards at flight level 340—about 34,000 feet—under instrument flight rules and receiving a radar control service from London Control.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.airproxboard.org.uk/uploadedFiles/Content/Standard_content/Airprox_report_files/2013/Airprox%20Report%202013086.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: airproxboard.org.uk">[airproxboard.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">airproxboard.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Airprox Report 2013086</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Airprox Report 2013086</span></span></span>

Some early newspaper reports gave 13 July as the date, but the primary Airprox document states **19 July 2013**. The official record should take precedence because it was compiled from the pilot’s report, radar recordings and the air traffic control investigation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ibtimes.co.uk/heathrow-jet-has-close-encounter-ufo-1431077" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ibtimes.co.uk">[International Business Times UK]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ibtimes.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">heathrow jet has close encounter ufo 1431077</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">International Business Times UKHeathrow Jet Has &#x27;Close Encounter&#x27; with UFO &#124; IBTimes UKJanuary 5, 2014 — Heathrow Jet Has &#x27;Close Encounte...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 5, 2014</span></span></span>

Immediately before the sighting, the first officer was looking down at the pilot’s log while the captain looked west through the left-hand direct-vision window. As the captain turned his gaze forwards, he perceived an object apparently approaching at roughly the aircraft’s own height, slightly above the cockpit windscreen. He believed there was no time to avoid it. His instinctive response was to duck to the right and reach across to alert the first officer rather than attempt a spoken warning.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.airproxboard.org.uk/uploadedFiles/Content/Standard_content/Airprox_report_files/2013/Airprox%20Report%202013086.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: airproxboard.org.uk">[airproxboard.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">airproxboard.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Airprox Report 2013086</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Airprox Report 2013086</span></span></span>

The captain expected an impact. Instead, he perceived the object passing only a few feet above the aircraft. He later described it as bright silver, metallic-looking and shaped like a cigar or rugby ball. The observation was extremely brief: there was no opportunity to establish its true size, distance, speed or construction. The first officer, who looked up only after seeing the captain react, saw nothing outside.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.airproxboard.org.uk/uploadedFiles/Content/Standard_content/Airprox_report_files/2013/Airprox%20Report%202013086.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: airproxboard.org.uk">[airproxboard.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">airproxboard.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Airprox Report 2013086</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Airprox Report 2013086</span></span></span>

Once the immediate shock had passed, the captain checked the Traffic Collision Avoidance System, or TCAS, and asked air traffic control about nearby aircraft. Neither check disclosed traffic capable of explaining what he thought had crossed the A320’s path. The crew agreed to file an Airprox report, and after landing the captain discussed the event at length with the air traffic control watch manager responsible for the sector.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.airproxboard.org.uk/uploadedFiles/Content/Standard_content/Airprox_report_files/2013/Airprox%20Report%202013086.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: airproxboard.org.uk">[airproxboard.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">airproxboard.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Airprox Report 2013086</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Airprox Report 2013086</span></span></span>

These actions strengthen the case that the captain genuinely believed something dangerous had happened. He did not merely report an unusual light long after the event; he checked the cockpit system, contacted controllers and entered the experience into a formal aviation-safety process. They do not, however, establish that the object was as close as it appeared. In a split-second observation against an open sky, perceived distance and speed depend heavily on assumptions about an object’s unknown size.

## What radar and traffic checks ruled out

The investigation was broader than many popular summaries suggest. The Civil Aviation Authority’s Air Traffic Standards Investigation team examined area radar recordings, the captain’s written report and an investigation conducted by NATS, the organisation providing air traffic services. The Military Radar Analysis Cell was also unable to trace the reported object.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.airproxboard.org.uk/uploadedFiles/Content/Standard_content/Airprox_report_files/2013/Airprox%20Report%202013086.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: airproxboard.org.uk">[airproxboard.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">airproxboard.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Airprox Report 2013086</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Airprox Report 2013086</span></span></span>

Radar did show one initially unidentified primary contact approximately 2.2 nautical miles from the A320’s position at the relevant time. Investigators traced it to a Tiger Moth aircraft flying at low level towards White Waltham airfield. Its route and altitude eliminated it as the object perceived near the A320 at 34,000 feet.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.airproxboard.org.uk/uploadedFiles/Content/Standard_content/Airprox_report_files/2013/Airprox%20Report%202013086.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: airproxboard.org.uk">[airproxboard.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">airproxboard.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Airprox Report 2013086</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Airprox Report 2013086</span></span></span>

Three high-altitude airliners were also identified:

* a Boeing 747 about 38 nautical miles west of the A320 at flight level 370;
* a Boeing 737 approximately 23 nautical miles to the north-west at flight level 358;
* another Boeing 737 roughly 28 nautical miles ahead, travelling at the same flight level on a similar route.

</div>

None was close enough to represent the head-on aircraft the captain thought he saw. Their presence nevertheless became important when investigators considered whether sunlight reflected from a distant aeroplane could have produced a misleading flash or shape.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.airproxboard.org.uk/uploadedFiles/Content/Standard_content/Airprox_report_files/2013/Airprox%20Report%202013086.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: airproxboard.org.uk">[airproxboard.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">airproxboard.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Airprox Report 2013086</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Airprox Report 2013086</span></span></span>

The absence of a TCAS return is useful but not decisive. TCAS tracks surrounding aircraft through replies from their transponders. It does not display a non-transponder object, an aircraft whose transponder is not responding, or an ordinary unpowered object such as a balloon. A blank display therefore strongly counts against a normally equipped airliner being feet away, but it cannot prove that the space outside the cockpit was empty.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.eurocontrol.int/system/acas" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: eurocontrol.int">[eurocontrol.int]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">eurocontrol.int</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">orne collision avoidance system Helping pilots avoid potential midair collisions by tracking other aircraft in the surrou...</span></span></span>

Ground radar has related limitations. Secondary surveillance depends on transponder replies, while primary radar detects reflected radio energy and may not reliably show every small, low-reflectivity or fleeting target. Even so, an ordinary aircraft large enough to resemble a serious collision threat at close range would normally be expected to leave more evidence than appeared here. Investigators found no immediate traffic return corresponding to the captain’s account.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.airproxboard.org.uk/uploadedFiles/Content/Standard_content/Airprox_report_files/2013/Airprox%20Report%202013086.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: airproxboard.org.uk">[airproxboard.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">airproxboard.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Airprox Report 2013086</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Airprox Report 2013086</span></span></span>

Balloon activity was checked separately. A pre-flight bulletin contained three notices covering possible meteorological or radiosonde balloon releases, but the organisations responsible confirmed that none had been launched from the specified sites that day. The Board also calculated that a helium envelope would need to be roughly one metre in diameter to reach flight level 340, making a commercially available toy balloon an unlikely explanation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.airproxboard.org.uk/uploadedFiles/Content/Standard_content/Airprox_report_files/2013/Airprox%20Report%202013086.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: airproxboard.org.uk">[airproxboard.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">airproxboard.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Airprox Report 2013086</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Airprox Report 2013086</span></span></span>

That conclusion is sometimes overstated as “all balloons were ruled out”. The investigation ruled out the notified meteorological launches and judged an ordinary toy balloon unsuitable for the altitude. It did not demonstrate that no unrecorded specialist balloon, lightweight debris or other non-transponder object could possibly have been present. There was simply no positive evidence for one.

## Could sunlight have created the encounter?

The Airprox report records the Sun at a bearing of 278 degrees and an elevation of about 24 degrees as viewed from the A320’s altitude. In practical terms, it was low in the western sky—the direction in which the captain had been looking before turning forwards. Investigators noted that the more distant aircraft to the west could have reflected sunlight.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.airproxboard.org.uk/uploadedFiles/Content/Standard_content/Airprox_report_files/2013/Airprox%20Report%202013086.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: airproxboard.org.uk">[airproxboard.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">airproxboard.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Airprox Report 2013086</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Airprox Report 2013086</span></span></span>

Board members proposed that a bright glint, combined with the captain’s head movement, might have created a powerful impression of an object moving rapidly towards and over the cockpit. The suggestion was not that the captain invented the sighting. It was that the visual system may have converted a brief, poorly referenced stimulus into an apparently solid object on a collision path.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.airproxboard.org.uk/uploadedFiles/Content/Standard_content/Airprox_report_files/2013/Airprox%20Report%202013086.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: airproxboard.org.uk">[airproxboard.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">airproxboard.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Airprox Report 2013086</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Airprox Report 2013086</span></span></span>

Several features make this plausible:

**The sighting began during a change of gaze.** The captain was turning from the side window towards the front. Apparent motion caused by eye or head movement can be difficult to separate from the movement of a briefly seen external light.

**There was no reliable scale.** Describing something as rugby-ball-shaped identifies its apparent outline, not its actual dimensions. A small nearby object and a bright reflection from a distant aircraft can occupy a similar angle in the observer’s vision.

**The object was seen by only one pilot.** The first officer was looking down and therefore cannot contradict the captain, but the absence of a second visual track leaves no independent estimate of position or movement.

**No impact evidence followed.** There was no reported strike, aircraft damage or recovered material, despite the captain’s impression that the object passed only feet above the fuselage.

The reflection hypothesis nevertheless has weaknesses. No particular aircraft was shown to produce the sighting, and the report did not reconstruct a precise optical path demonstrating how a reflection would appear cigar-shaped or seem to cross directly overhead. The Board therefore described the idea as having merit but explicitly treated it as supposition rather than a solved explanation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.airproxboard.org.uk/uploadedFiles/Content/Standard_content/Airprox_report_files/2013/Airprox%20Report%202013086.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: airproxboard.org.uk">[airproxboard.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">airproxboard.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Airprox Report 2013086</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Airprox Report 2013086</span></span></span>

## Why the Airprox case remains unresolved

The official cause was recorded simply as a **“sighting report”**, with risk category D. In the Airprox system, category D does not mean that a dangerous collision was confirmed. It means the risk could not be determined because the available information was insufficient, inconclusive or conflicting.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.airproxboard.org.uk/uploadedFiles/Content/Standard_content/Airprox_report_files/2013/Airprox%20Report%202013086.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: airproxboard.org.uk">[airproxboard.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">airproxboard.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Airprox Report 2013086</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Airprox Report 2013086</span></span></span>

This distinction is central to understanding the case. The captain’s fear was real, but the reported separation of a few feet was a perception, not a measured distance. There was no radar track for a close object, no TCAS target, no second visual witness, no photograph, no physical trace and no independently established trajectory. The Board could neither identify an intruder nor calculate an actual closest point of approach.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.airproxboard.org.uk/uploadedFiles/Content/Standard_content/Airprox_report_files/2013/Airprox%20Report%202013086.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: airproxboard.org.uk">[airproxboard.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">airproxboard.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Airprox Report 2013086</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Airprox Report 2013086</span></span></span>

The investigation did establish several narrower conclusions:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--comparison" markdown="1">

* no known aircraft was flying in the A320’s immediate vicinity;
* the low-level Tiger Moth seen on radar was unrelated;
* the advertised meteorological balloon launches did not take place;
* a normal toy balloon was considered unlikely to reach the reported altitude;
* no military radar trace provided an alternative identification;
* sunlight reflecting from distant traffic remained plausible but unproven.

</div>

An unresolved finding therefore reflects an evidence gap, not a finding that conventional explanations were impossible. This is the “missing explanation” at the heart of the case: investigators could offer a credible perceptual mechanism, but lacked enough data to test it properly or to replace it with a confirmed physical object.

Later reporting did little to strengthen the original claim. Newspaper accounts publicised the captain’s vivid description and often presented the lack of identification as the main result. Some also introduced errors, including the wrong incident date, or used “UFO” as though it described a distinct craft rather than an unidentified observation. The surviving official report remains the principal evidence and contains no later corroborating witness, instrument detection or recovered object.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ibtimes.co.uk/heathrow-jet-has-close-encounter-ufo-1431077" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ibtimes.co.uk">[International Business Times UK]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ibtimes.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">heathrow jet has close encounter ufo 1431077</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">International Business Times UKHeathrow Jet Has &#x27;Close Encounter&#x27; with UFO &#124; IBTimes UKJanuary 5, 2014 — Heathrow Jet Has &#x27;Close Encounte...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 5, 2014</span></span></span>

## What the encounter adds to Berkshire’s UFO history

Within Berkshire’s UFO record, the encounter stands out because it passed through established flight-safety channels rather than surviving only as a press story or retrospective witness account. The captain reported promptly, controllers were consulted, radar data were preserved, balloon notices were checked and the circumstances were discussed by a specialist board. An Airprox is investigated because a pilot or controller believes aircraft safety may have been compromised; it is not an official classification of something as extraterrestrial or technologically extraordinary.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.airproxboard.org.uk/learn-more-about-airprox/frequently-asked-questions/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: airproxboard.org.uk">[airproxboard.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">airproxboard.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">In the judgement of the air traffic controllers who reported the event it was an Airprox and was therefore fully investigated and assesse...</span></span></span>

The case is consequently stronger than a typical unsupported light-in-the-sky report, but weaker than its dramatic language initially suggests. The witness was an experienced professional in a position to recognise ordinary aircraft, yet even skilled pilots can be misled by extremely brief observations with no scale, stable reference or corroborating instrument return. Conversely, the lack of radar or TCAS evidence cannot by itself exclude every small or non-transponder object.

The most defensible assessment is that the captain underwent a sincere and alarming visual encounter whose source remains undetermined. A sun-glint and movement-induced illusion is the leading explanation recorded by the investigators, but it was never demonstrated. The case remains officially unresolved because the evidence was too limited to identify either a real airborne object or a specific optical effect—not because the investigation uncovered evidence of an exotic craft.
