## Introduction

Ronald Wildman’s encounter remains one of [Buckinghamshire]({{ 'what-really-happened-in-buckinghamshires/' | relative_url }})’s most interesting early UFO reports, but not because later research has proved that an extraordinary craft followed his car. Its importance lies in the unusually prompt reporting, the police contact and the surviving contemporary investigation. At about 3.30am on 9 February 1962, the Luton delivery driver said that a low, luminous object moved ahead of his vehicle on the road towards Aylesbury, apparently restricting its speed before accelerating away. Investigators found him sincere, yet found no second witness, measurable vehicle damage or physical trace.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ufomagazines.com/bufora-1959-1963-luforo-bulletin-vols1-to-4-luforo-bulletin-vol-3-no-01-jan-feb-1962/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufomagazines.com">[UFO Magazine Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufomagazines.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFO Magazine ArchiveLUFORO BULLETIN — LONDON UNIDENTIFIED FLYING OBJECT RESEARCH ORGANIZATION &#124; UFO Magazine Archiveon their knowledge an...</span></span></span>

Reassessment therefore leaves the case in a narrow but significant category: stronger than a late, anonymous tale, but far short of proof that a solid unknown machine was present. The surviving record supports the conclusion that Wildman underwent a disturbing experience and reported it quickly. It does not establish what caused that experience, or even confirm that every detail preserved in later retellings formed part of his earliest account.

## What happened on the road?

Wildman was a vehicle delivery driver taking a new Vauxhall from the Luton area to Swansea. Contemporary private investigators gave his age as 34 and described the vehicle as a Vauxhall Victor. He was travelling through the road network around Ivinghoe, Tring and Aylesbury when he noticed what he took to be a large object above or immediately ahead of the road. The event is normally placed within Buckinghamshire because its reported course continued towards Aylesbury, although parts of the journey lay close to the Hertfordshire boundary.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ufomagazines.com/bufora-1959-1963-luforo-bulletin-vols1-to-4-luforo-bulletin-vol-3-no-01-jan-feb-1962/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufomagazines.com">[UFO Magazine Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufomagazines.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFO Magazine ArchiveLUFORO BULLETIN — LONDON UNIDENTIFIED FLYING OBJECT RESEARCH ORGANIZATION &#124; UFO Magazine Archiveon their knowledge an...</span></span></span>

According to the account published by the London Unidentified Flying Object Research Organisation, or LUFORO, the object appeared about 30 to 40 feet wide. It had a curved upper surface, a straight lower edge and a row of dark, roughly square markings that resembled windows or portholes. Wildman described it as luminous but apparently silent, although he acknowledged that the sound of his own car might have masked any noise.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ufomagazines.com/bufora-1959-1963-luforo-bulletin-vols1-to-4-luforo-bulletin-vol-3-no-01-jan-feb-1962/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufomagazines.com">[UFO Magazine Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufomagazines.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFO Magazine ArchiveLUFORO BULLETIN — LONDON UNIDENTIFIED FLYING OBJECT RESEARCH ORGANIZATION &#124; UFO Magazine Archiveon their knowledge an...</span></span></span>

The most striking part of the report was the apparent interaction with the vehicle. Wildman said that the object travelled ahead at roughly 20 mph and that he could not make the car go faster. After a short interval it crossed trees beside the road, whereupon frost or ice fell onto the car “like rain or hail”. A ring of vapour reportedly formed beneath the object before it accelerated forwards, upwards and to the right, disappearing at what he considered extraordinary speed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ufomagazines.com/bufora-1959-1963-luforo-bulletin-vols1-to-4-luforo-bulletin-vol-3-no-01-jan-feb-1962/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufomagazines.com">[UFO Magazine Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufomagazines.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFO Magazine ArchiveLUFORO BULLETIN — LONDON UNIDENTIFIED FLYING OBJECT RESEARCH ORGANIZATION &#124; UFO Magazine Archiveon their knowledge an...</span></span></span>

There is an important discrepancy over distance. The LUFORO report says that the object remained ahead for approximately 50 yards. The account recorded through the police and later quoted from an RAF Provost and Security Services report describes Wildman being slowed from about 40 mph to 20 mph over 400 yards. Those figures cannot simply be combined into one seamless reconstruction. They may refer to different phases of the incident, but they may also reflect imperfect note-taking, press compression or the normal variation that appears when a fast-moving experience is retold to several audiences.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ufomagazines.com/bufora-1959-1963-luforo-bulletin-vols1-to-4-luforo-bulletin-vol-3-no-01-jan-feb-1962/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufomagazines.com">[UFO Magazine Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufomagazines.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFO Magazine ArchiveLUFORO BULLETIN — LONDON UNIDENTIFIED FLYING OBJECT RESEARCH ORGANIZATION &#124; UFO Magazine Archiveon their knowledge an...</span></span></span>

## Why the immediate report matters

Wildman did not wait years before telling the story. He contacted the police shortly after the encounter, and the incident was entered in the Aylesbury station occurrence book. A patrol car was reportedly sent to the area but found no object or other obvious evidence. Sergeant Schofield and a constable later told an RAF investigator that Wildman had appeared genuinely shaken and that they did not regard his story as an invention.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.fringearchives.com/ronald-wildmans-1962-ufo-sighting-in-aylesbury/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: fringearchives.com">[Fringe Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">fringearchives.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Calloway Leave a Comment Ronald Wildman’s UFO sighting on February 9, 196</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Fringe ArchivesRonald Wildman’s 1962 UFO Sighting in AylesburyRonald Wildman’s 1962 UFO Sighting in Aylesbury Ronald Wildman’s 1962 UFO S...</span></span></span>

That sequence improves the case in several ways. It reduces the opportunity for long-term memory distortion; it places the claim in a dated official record; and it shows that Wildman was prepared to face questioning at a time when reporting a “flying saucer” could invite ridicule. His occupation also matters modestly. A delivery driver familiar with vehicles and early-morning roads was not an inexperienced observer suddenly placed behind a steering wheel. None of that proves his interpretation, but it makes casual fabrication less persuasive as a complete explanation.

Police confidence should nevertheless be understood correctly. The officers were judging Wildman’s demeanour and apparent honesty, not certifying the physical existence of the object. A witness can be sober, distressed and completely sincere while still misjudging distance, size, movement or the relationship between an unfamiliar light and a vehicle’s behaviour. The police record is evidence that a prompt complaint was made; it is not independent observation of the alleged craft.

## What did the investigators actually find?

A private party including Dr B. E. Finch, R. R. Russell and Dr G. G. Doel interviewed Wildman and examined the reported route. They searched for marks or other traces and tested for unusual magnetism and radioactivity. Their tests were negative. Enquiries among local residents produced no corroborating witness, despite the object’s reported size, low altitude and rapid departure.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ufomagazines.com/bufora-1959-1963-luforo-bulletin-vols1-to-4-luforo-bulletin-vol-3-no-01-jan-feb-1962/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufomagazines.com">[UFO Magazine Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufomagazines.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFO Magazine ArchiveLUFORO BULLETIN — LONDON UNIDENTIFIED FLYING OBJECT RESEARCH ORGANIZATION &#124; UFO Magazine Archiveon their knowledge an...</span></span></span>

The investigators concluded that Wildman was truthful and had seen a flying object of unknown type and origin. That conclusion reflected their assessment of the witness, but it exceeded what their physical examination had established. They had confirmed neither a structured vehicle nor an anomalous force. Their search instead produced a useful set of negative findings: no landing mark, no detectable radiation, no magnetic anomaly and no supporting observer.

The Air Ministry also became aware of the case. The relevant official material survives within National Archives file AIR 2/16918, a collection of reports and correspondence concerning unidentified flying objects from 1961 to 1963. On 16 February, Sergeant C. J. Perry of the RAF Provost and Security Services visited Aylesbury police and recorded what officers had been told. His involvement demonstrates that the report entered official channels, but official attention should not be confused with official endorsement. Ministries investigated unidentified reports partly to determine whether they represented aircraft, security problems or hazards; opening or retaining a file did not mean that officials believed an extraterrestrial craft was involved.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://discovery.nationalarchives.gov.uk/details/record?catid=3387746&amp;catln=6" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: discovery.nationalarchives.gov.uk">[Discovery]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">discovery.nationalarchives.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Discovery AIR 2</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Unidentified Flying Objects (UFOs): sightings; reports by members of public &#124; The National ArchivesAIR 2. The documents support a more restrained reading: the police had logged an unusual roadside report, the Air Ministry sought details, and no decisive official explanation or physical confirmation followed.

## Did the object really affect the car?

The alleged loss of speed is the feature that most clearly separates Wildman’s story from an ordinary report of a distant light. If an external force physically held a new car to 20 mph despite continued acceleration, that would be potentially measurable evidence. Yet the surviving accounts do not include a mechanic’s inspection, an identified fault, instrument readings, tyre marks, engine damage or later repetition of the problem.

The wording also leaves uncertainty about what Wildman experienced. “The car would not go faster” is not necessarily the same as confirmed engine failure. On an unfamiliar road in darkness, a driver may slow instinctively while concentrating on something ahead. Gradient, a bend, frost, poor traction or a change of gear could all affect speed without being recognised in the moment. The account does not preserve enough technical detail to distinguish mechanical resistance from cautious or involuntary deceleration.

This does not imply that Wildman knowingly exaggerated the effect. A driver who believes a large object is pacing him may naturally connect every change in engine note, road speed or acceleration with that object. Because no instrumented examination was conducted immediately, the claimed vehicle effect remains testimony rather than independent physical evidence.

## Could cloud, fog or ice explain the sighting?

A contemporary mundane suggestion was that Wildman had seen a low cloud illuminated by his headlights. In its simplest form, that explanation is incomplete. Ordinary cloud would not literally contain portholes, pace a car and then accelerate away. Fog also alters a driver’s sense of motion. The Met Office warns that it can create the illusion that a vehicle is travelling in slow motion. That is relevant because Wildman’s report combined an apparently nearby luminous form with the sensation that his car had been restrained. Reduced peripheral detail could have made both the object’s distance and his own speed harder to judge.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://weather.metoffice.gov.uk/warnings-and-advice/seasonal-advice/travel/advice-for-travelling-in-fog" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: weather.metoffice.gov.uk">[Met Office]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">weather.metoffice.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Met Office Advice for travelling in fog</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Fog can drift rapidly and is often patchy. The reported fall of frost is equally ambiguous. It could be interpreted as matter displaced by a low object passing through treetops. It could also indicate freezing fog, accumulated frost or ice shaken from branches by wind or by the passage of the car. Freezing fog consists of supercooled droplets that freeze on contact with roads, trees and vehicles, so ice on the windscreen is not by itself evidence of an unusual craft.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://weather.metoffice.gov.uk/warnings-and-advice/seasonal-advice/travel/advice-for-travelling-in-fog" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: weather.metoffice.gov.uk">[Met Office]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">weather.metoffice.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Met Office Advice for travelling in fog</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Fog can drift rapidly and is often patchy. ## What other explanations remain possible?

An aircraft or helicopter is difficult to exclude absolutely, but the description does not fit one neatly. Wildman reported no conventional navigation lights or engine sound, and the object supposedly remained exceptionally low. Size and altitude estimates made at night without a known reference, however, are highly uncertain. A distant aircraft seen through mist can appear larger, slower or lower than it is, especially when its apparent movement is compared with a moving car.

A balloon or astronomical object explains even less of the complete narrative. A planet or bright star could appear to remain in roughly the same position while a car followed a winding road, but it would not readily produce the reported broad lower edge, markings, vapour ring or close passage over trees. A balloon could drift silently but would not be expected to accelerate sharply. These explanations become workable only by treating several of the most dramatic details as misperceptions.

A hoax cannot be disproved, but the early police report and Wildman’s visible distress give it little positive support. No clear motive, accomplice or fabricated physical evidence emerged. The more economical sceptical position is not that he invented everything, but that a sincere witness constructed a solid, nearby object from confusing visual and driving cues in difficult conditions.

The possibility of an experimental aircraft is sometimes raised because Cold War Britain contained numerous aviation and defence establishments. No identified project has been linked to the incident, however, and secrecy alone cannot fill that evidential gap. The case provides no radar record, flight log, military witness or technical description that points to a specific British aircraft.

## Did later reporting strengthen the case?

Later publicity preserved the story but did not materially strengthen it. The most dependable elements remain those closest to February 1962: the police entry, the RAF investigator’s summary and the LUFORO field report. Later accounts add colour, including more precise heights, durations, vehicle descriptions and interpretations of official interest, yet these details are not always consistent. Those interpretations go beyond the contemporary findings.

What later access to official files has genuinely added is confirmation that the police and Air Ministry did not ignore the report. It establishes a documentary chain that many local UFO stories lack. It has not produced photographs, a recovered object, a second witness, radar confirmation or a technical examination of the car.

## How strong is the Wildman case today?

The Wildman encounter should be classified as unresolved but weakly corroborated. Its strongest evidence is the prompt and apparently sincere testimony of a named witness, supported by police confirmation that he was distressed and reported the event immediately. Its weakest point is that every extraordinary feature depends on that single witness’s perception during a pre-dawn drive.

The negative investigation matters as much as the dramatic story. Nothing abnormal was found along the route, no local observer confirmed a large low-flying object, and no lasting effect on the Vauxhall was documented. Cloud, mist, ice, altered speed perception and an ordinary light source can account for parts of the experience, although no single conventional explanation reproduces every reported detail.

Within Buckinghamshire’s UFO history, the case is valuable less as evidence of alien visitation than as an unusually clear example of how a sincere close-encounter report can remain unsettled. It was reported promptly enough to deserve attention, investigated seriously enough to survive in both private and official records, but preserved too incompletely to decide whether Wildman saw an unfamiliar aircraft, misread a complex combination of weather and light, or encountered something that was never identified.
