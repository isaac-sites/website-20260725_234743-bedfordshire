## Introduction

The waves of orange lights reported over [Hertfordshire]({{ 'what-really-flew-over-hertfordshire/' | relative_url }}) in the late 2000s were striking, but the best-supported explanation for many of them is mass releases of sky lanterns rather than unusual aircraft. The strongest examples share a recognisable pattern: several warm orange lights, little or no sound, slow movement, loose lines or clusters, and lights fading separately rather than departing together. These are precisely the characteristics that the Ministry of Defence and The National Archives later associated with the nationwide lantern craze.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cdn.nationalarchives.gov.uk/documents/ufo-highlights-guide-2013.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cdn.nationalarchives.gov.uk">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cdn.nationalarchives.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ufo highlights guide 2013</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesUFO highlights guide 2013...</span></span></span>



<img src="{{ "/assets/images/Hertfordshire_38dc7a_orange_lights_and_la_104c20-overview.webp" | relative_url }}" alt="Overview image for Orange Lights" loading="eager" decoding="sync" fetchpriority="high">

That does not prove that every orange light was a lantern. The official entries are often too brief to establish wind direction, duration, angular speed or the presence of aircraft navigation lights. Yet the Hemel Hempstead, Hitchin and Cheshunt reports sit within a much wider British surge in similar sightings, making a shared, ordinary mechanism more persuasive than a sequence of unrelated extraordinary craft.

## The Hemel Hempstead, Hitchin and Cheshunt reports

Hertfordshire’s orange-light sequence was already visible in the Ministry of Defence log during 2007. A report dated 6 August recorded three bright orange lights over Hemel Hempstead. Another entry, received on 26 July but lacking a firm sighting date, described three orange lights travelling in a line over Hitchin. Neither summary records sound, duration, weather, changes of direction or supporting photographs.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/government/uploads/system/uploads/attachment_data/file/16852/ufo_report_2007.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: assets.publishing.service.gov.uk">[GOV.UK Assets]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">assets.publishing.service.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UK Assets</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UK Assets</span></span></span>

These two reports matter less because of any dramatic manoeuvre than because of their similarity. Three orange points in a line can give the immediate impression of a single organised formation. Without visible bodies between them, however, there is no evidence that the lights were physically connected. Three independently drifting lanterns released seconds apart can preserve rough spacing for part of a flight, especially when carried by the same layer of air.

The most revealing Hertfordshire entry came from Cheshunt in 2008. The witness reported about 30 orange lights a few hundred feet above the ground, moving slowly in a straight line. The message was received on 28 August, although the table gives no firm date or time for the observation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/5a789e38ed915d042206403a/ufo_report_2008.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: assets.publishing.service.gov.uk">[GOV.UK Assets]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">assets.publishing.service.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UK Assets</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UK Assets</span></span></span> This is much harder to reconcile with ordinary aircraft: 30 separate aircraft would be expected to display regulated navigation or anti-collision lights, produce noise under many viewing conditions and maintain controlled separation rather than resemble identical orange points.

It is, by contrast, a strong descriptive match for a group lantern release. The number of lights suggests an event rather than a solitary misidentification, while their common colour, low apparent height, slow pace and shared track are consistent with small hot-air balloons entering the same wind stream. The record does not identify a wedding, festival or private celebration in the Cheshunt area, so the lantern conclusion remains a probability rather than a demonstrated identification.

A further Cheshunt report at 01:50 on 18 July 2009 concerned one bright orange light that appeared to hover, became pinker and redder, then disappeared.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/5a7582c440f0b6397f35efcb/ufo_report_2009.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: assets.publishing.service.gov.uk">[GOV.UK Assets]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">assets.publishing.service.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UK Assets</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UK Assets</span></span></span> A changing warm colour and gradual extinction can occur as a lantern’s flame weakens, turns relative to the observer or becomes obscured by haze. Apparent hovering is also weak evidence of controlled flight: an object moving mainly towards or away from a witness may show little sideways motion, particularly when distance and scale cannot be judged.

The Hitchin entry in the 2009 log is different and should not be folded casually into the lantern group. It describes a large round object reportedly circled by two aircraft and even records the witness’s suggestion that it might have been a natural event. It was reported at 12:48, in daylight, and contains no orange-light description.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/5a7582c440f0b6397f35efcb/ufo_report_2009.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: assets.publishing.service.gov.uk">[GOV.UK Assets]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">assets.publishing.service.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UK Assets</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UK Assets</span></span></span> It therefore belongs to Hertfordshire’s broader UFO record, but it does not strengthen the case for a county-wide wave of nocturnal orange craft.

## How lanterns create convincing formations

A sky lantern is a lightweight paper envelope lifted by air heated by a small fuel cell. Once released, it rises until lift, cooling and atmospheric movement determine its path. It has no steering system. Its horizontal motion is therefore governed chiefly by the air mass around it, while buoyancy produces a slower vertical component. Experimental and modelling work has found that lanterns can reach several hundred metres and remain airborne for minutes, enough time to cross a substantial portion of a town’s visible sky.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://pure.tudelft.nl/ws/files/20215986/AIAA_Skylantern_paper_version_TUDELFT.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: pure.tudelft.nl">[TU Delft Research Portal]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">pure.tudelft.nl</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">In many countries, like The Netherlands, the regulator has chosen to put rules in place to warn the...</span></span></span>

Several features make a release look more organised than it is.

**A shared wind creates a common track.** Lanterns launched from one site enter broadly similar airflow, so they can travel in the same direction at comparable speeds. A sequence released at short intervals naturally becomes a line or “convoy”. A cluster released together may spread into an arc, triangle or uneven grid as small differences in lift and local turbulence alter the spacing.

**Perspective turns depth into pattern.** Witnesses normally lack reliable information about the lights’ distance or altitude. Objects separated along the line of sight can appear side by side, while three lights almost inevitably suggest a triangle. As the observer, lanterns and wind all occupy different positions, the apparent shape can contract, rotate or stretch without any object making a powered turn.

**Individual flames make the lights seem responsive.** A lantern can brighten, flicker or dim as its fuel burns unevenly, the paper rotates, or haze passes between it and the observer. If one fades while others remain visible, witnesses may interpret the change as a light switching off, an object accelerating away or part of a formation breaking ranks.

**Silence increases the mystery.** Aircraft can sometimes be heard only after they have crossed the observer, but a nearby formation of numerous conventional aircraft would usually provide additional clues: engine noise, strobes, red and green navigation lights or clearly changing geometry. Lanterns produce no engine sound and generally show a steady amber, orange or reddish glow.

The National Archives explicitly connected the national rise in reports during 2008 and 2009 with lantern releases at weddings and public holidays. Its review notes that many people filmed formations of orange lights, found them frightening or astonishing, and believed that the objects were moving under control. It also records that sightings clustered during summer evenings, when more people were outside at barbecues or social gatherings and lantern releases were more likely to be noticed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cdn.nationalarchives.gov.uk/documents/ufo-highlights-guide-2013.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cdn.nationalarchives.gov.uk">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cdn.nationalarchives.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ufo highlights guide 2013</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesUFO highlights guide 2013...</span></span></span>

The wider official logs reinforce that interpretation. Across Britain, entries describe dozens of orange lights travelling slowly, forming lines, arriving in groups and extinguishing one after another. In 2007, for example, the MoD recorded 60 orange glowing lights moving slowly in a trail over Uckfield, and 20 flickering orange lights appearing and fading over Trowbridge.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/government/uploads/system/uploads/attachment_data/file/16852/ufo_report_2007.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: assets.publishing.service.gov.uk">[GOV.UK Assets]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">assets.publishing.service.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UK Assets</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UK Assets</span></span></span> In 2008, reports included fleets of orange lights over Bristol, groups in formation over London and 30 or more orange-yellow lights arriving in batches near Hainault.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/5a789e38ed915d042206403a/ufo_report_2008.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: assets.publishing.service.gov.uk">[GOV.UK Assets]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">assets.publishing.service.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UK Assets</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UK Assets</span></span></span> Hertfordshire’s reports were therefore part of a national visual pattern, not an isolated county anomaly.

## Which details would distinguish lanterns from aircraft?

Colour alone is not enough. Aircraft can appear orange through haze or when seen near the horizon, while lanterns may look white in photographs that overexpose the flame. A useful assessment depends on several details considered together.

**Evidence favouring lanterns includes:**


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* a constant amber, orange or fire-like glow;
* gentle flickering rather than regular flashing;
* several lights following broadly the same drift;
* a loose line, cluster or changing geometric pattern;
* no visible red-green navigation-light pairing;
* gradual fading, especially one object at a time;
* movement broadly consistent with the wind;
* a sighting near a celebration, wedding venue or public holiday.

</div>

**Evidence favouring aircraft includes:**


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* regular white anti-collision strobes;
* identifiable red and green navigation lights;
* repeated passage along an established route;
* engine or rotor noise consistent with apparent distance;
* coordinated turns with stable spacing;
* radar, air-traffic or flight-tracking corroboration;
* a visible fuselage or wing shape through binoculars.

</div>

The Civil Aviation Authority treats sky lanterns as objects capable of affecting UK airspace and provides guidance for organisers planning releases. That is important when judging witness estimates. A light described as being at “aircraft height” is not necessarily an aircraft, because lanterns can rise into the lower levels used by aeroplanes and helicopters, particularly around take-off and landing routes.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.caa.co.uk/data-and-publications/publications/documents/content/cap-736/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: caa.co.uk">[Civil Aviation Authority]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">caa.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Civil Aviation AuthorityCAP 736: Operation of Directed Light, Fireworks, Toy Balloons and Sky Lanterns within UK Airspace &#124; UK Civil Avia...</span></span></span>

Claims that lights travelled “against the wind” require particular caution. Surface wind observed in a garden is not necessarily the same as the wind several hundred feet above it. Direction and speed can change with height. A useful investigation would therefore need the sighting’s precise time and location, weather observations at relevant altitudes and an estimated track across fixed landmarks. None of those details appears in the short Hemel Hempstead, Hitchin or 2008 Cheshunt summaries.

Speed estimates are equally fragile. A witness cannot calculate true speed without knowing distance. A small lantern nearby and a large aircraft much farther away can have similar apparent angular motion. Descriptions such as “slow”, “helicopter speed” or “a few hundred feet up” are valuable impressions, but they are not measurements unless supported by timed movement between known reference points.

## Why the reports remain useful

The lantern explanation weakens the extraordinary interpretation of Hertfordshire’s orange-light wave, but it does not make the reports worthless. They show how a novel object can generate repeated sincere sightings when many observers encounter it without a familiar frame of reference. The witnesses did not need to be careless or fanciful: a silent procession of glowing points, seen without visible envelopes or a known launch site, can be genuinely difficult to identify.

They also expose the limitations of the MoD lists. The tables recorded what people said they saw, but usually did not document a complete investigation. Inclusion in the official file therefore confirms that a report was received, not that an anomalous vehicle was verified. The National Archives found that the national intake rose from an average of about 150 reports a year during 2000–07 to 208 in 2008 and 643 by 30 November 2009. It attributed a substantial share of the increase to lantern sightings, alongside greater publicity surrounding the release of government UFO files.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cdn.nationalarchives.gov.uk/documents/ufo-highlights-guide-2013.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cdn.nationalarchives.gov.uk">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cdn.nationalarchives.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ufo highlights guide 2013</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesUFO highlights guide 2013...</span></span></span>

For Hertfordshire, the most defensible conclusion is graded rather than absolute. The 2007 Hemel Hempstead and Hitchin reports are weakly documented but compatible with lanterns. The 30-light Cheshunt procession in 2008 is strongly suggestive of a mass release, although no launch event is identified in the surviving summary. The single Cheshunt light in 2009 is also compatible with a lantern but lacks enough information for certainty. None of these entries contains the radar confirmation, close-range structural detail, independent aviation record or documented high-performance movement needed to make an unconventional-aircraft explanation more persuasive than windborne lights.

      button.setAttribute('aria-pressed', active ? 'true' : 'false');
    });
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-selected-market', marketId);
    if (persist) {
      try { if (window.localStorage) localStorage.setItem('phoenix-ebay-market', marketId); } catch (err) {}
    }
    closeMenu(section);
  }
  sections.forEach(function (section) {
    if (section.getAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init') === '1') return;
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init', '1');
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    var marketId = defaultMarket;
    if (window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation && window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket) {
      marketId = window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket(available, {
        storageKey: 'phoenix-ebay-market',
        defaultMarket: defaultMarket
      });
    } else if (available.indexOf(defaultMarket)< 0) {
      marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    }
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      select.addEventListener('change', function () {
        applyMarket(section, select.value, true);
      });
    }
    var trigger = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-trigger]');
    var menu = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-menu]');
    if (trigger && menu) {
      trigger.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
        event.stopPropagation();
        var expanded = trigger.getAttribute('aria-expanded') === 'true';
        menu.hidden = expanded;
        trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', expanded ? 'false' : 'true');
        var picker = trigger.closest('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
        if (picker) picker.classList.toggle('fr-ebay-market-picker--open', !expanded);
      });
      Array.prototype.slice.call(menu.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
        button.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
          event.stopPropagation();
          applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option'), true);
        });
      });
      document.addEventListener('click', function () { closeMenu(section); });
    }
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.addEventListener('click', function () {
        applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'), true);
      });
    });
    applyMarket(section, marketId, false);
  });
