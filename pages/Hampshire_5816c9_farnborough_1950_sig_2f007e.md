## Introduction

The Farnborough sightings of August and September 1950 still matter because they brought an experienced RAF test pilot, several service witnesses and Britain’s first official “flying saucer” investigators into the same case. Flight Lieutenant Stan Hubbard described a disc-like object over the Royal Aircraft Establishment, then reported a second observation from the airfield watchtower with five other officers. Officials accepted that the witnesses were sincere, but concluded that they had probably misperceived conventional aircraft.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cdn.nationalarchives.gov.uk/documents/the-ufo-files-extract.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cdn.nationalarchives.gov.uk">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cdn.nationalarchives.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Layout 1</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Archives Layout 1</span></span></span>

That leaves Farnborough unresolved in a limited sense: no surviving evidence identifies exactly what was seen. It does not mean that an extraordinary craft was proved to exist. The surviving case consists mainly of recollections and a short official summary. There are no known photographs, instrument readings, radar tracks, recovered materials or complete sets of independent witness statements. The case is historically strong but scientifically weak—a landmark in [Hampshire]({{ 'what-really-happened-in-hampshires-skies/' | relative_url }})’s UFO history because of who reported it and how government responded, not because it produced decisive physical evidence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://disclosdex.com/programs/1950-flying-saucer-working-party" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: disclosdex.com">[Disclosdex]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">disclosdex.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Flying Saucer Working Party &#124; Disclosdexreports as they appeared, report to DSI/JTIC when necessary, and keep in touch with Ame...</span></span></span>

## What Stan Hubbard reported

Hubbard was based at the Royal Aircraft Establishment at Farnborough, then one of Britain’s most important centres for aeronautical research and flight testing. That setting matters: he was accustomed to seeing aircraft, including experimental types, and worked among people professionally concerned with aviation. It also meant that Farnborough’s skies were unusually busy and could contain unfamiliar aircraft operating in unusual attitudes or lighting conditions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cdn.nationalarchives.gov.uk/documents/the-ufo-files-extract.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cdn.nationalarchives.gov.uk">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cdn.nationalarchives.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Layout 1</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Archives Layout 1</span></span></span>

The official Flying Saucer Working Party report dates Hubbard’s first sighting to **14 August 1950 at 11.27 am**. A later account published by The National Archives gives **15 August**, apparently reflecting Hubbard’s much later recollection. The difference is small, but it illustrates a central problem with the evidence: details preserved in contemporary official summaries do not always match those given in interviews more than half a century afterwards. The earlier official date should therefore be preferred while acknowledging the discrepancy.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ufotransparency.com/files/intl-uk-defe-44-119-policy-defe-44-119-dsac-ufo-policy" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufotransparency.com">[UFO Transparency]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufotransparency.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFO TransparencyDEFE 44/119, Defence Scientific Advisory Council UFO Policy, UK Ministry of Defence / Defence Scientific Advisory Council...</span></span></span>

According to the working party, Hubbard and two other officers heard a subdued humming noise and searched the sky. Only Hubbard reported seeing the object; the official account noted that he alone was wearing sunglasses. He described a flat, pearl-grey disc, apparently moving almost directly overhead. The report recorded an estimated diameter of about 50 feet, an altitude near 5,000 feet and a high apparent speed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ufotransparency.com/files/intl-uk-defe-44-119-policy-defe-44-119-dsac-ufo-policy" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufotransparency.com">[UFO Transparency]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufotransparency.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFO TransparencyDEFE 44/119, Defence Scientific Advisory Council UFO Policy, UK Ministry of Defence / Defence Scientific Advisory Council...</span></span></span>

Hubbard’s later description was more vivid and differed substantially in some measurements. He recalled an object resembling a discus viewed edge-on, rocking slightly while maintaining a straight course. He described a growing electrical hum, crackling or hissing, sparkling effects around the rim and a strong smell like ozone. He estimated that it might have been 700 to 1,000 feet above the ground, perhaps 100 feet across and travelling at 500 to 900 mph. Crucially, he also admitted that its lack of recognisable features made size, distance and speed difficult to judge.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cdn.nationalarchives.gov.uk/documents/the-ufo-files-extract.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cdn.nationalarchives.gov.uk">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cdn.nationalarchives.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Layout 1</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Archives Layout 1</span></span></span>

That admission is important. A witness cannot calculate the physical size or speed of an unidentified object from angular appearance alone unless its distance is independently known. A small object nearby and a large object far away can occupy the same apparent width in the sky. Hubbard’s figures were therefore impressions derived from assumed distance, not measurements comparable to radar data or calibrated photography.

The sensory richness of his later account—the sound, sparkling edge and ozone smell—makes the experience compelling, but it also raises evidential questions. Those details are prominent in Hubbard’s 2002 interview yet are not all reproduced in the working party’s brief contemporary summary. This does not show that they were invented; the original interview notes may have contained more than the final report. It does mean that the strongest dramatic details reach the public through late testimony rather than a surviving statement written at the time.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cdn.nationalarchives.gov.uk/documents/the-ufo-files-extract.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cdn.nationalarchives.gov.uk">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cdn.nationalarchives.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Layout 1</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Archives Layout 1</span></span></span>

## The second sighting over Farnborough

On 5 September 1950, during preparations for the Farnborough air show, Hubbard was on the airfield watchtower with five other RAF officers awaiting a display by the Hawker P.1081 experimental aircraft. At 4.09 pm, the group saw a distant object to the south, apparently over the Guildford–Farnham area. The official report described it as a light pearl-grey, flat disc with an apparent size comparable to a shirt button held at ordinary viewing distance.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ufotransparency.com/files/intl-uk-defe-44-119-policy-defe-44-119-dsac-ufo-policy" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufotransparency.com">[UFO Transparency]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufotransparency.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFO TransparencyDEFE 44/119, Defence Scientific Advisory Council UFO Policy, UK Ministry of Defence / Defence Scientific Advisory Council...</span></span></span>

The witnesses reported an unusual sequence of movements: a fluttering or “falling-leaf” descent, rapid horizontal motion, another fluttering climb and further fast movement before the object disappeared towards the horizon. Hubbard later said the display continued for about ten minutes and that the number of onlookers grew beyond a dozen. No one obtained a photograph, despite the sighting occurring at Britain’s leading aircraft-research airfield shortly before a major aviation exhibition.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cdn.nationalarchives.gov.uk/documents/the-ufo-files-extract.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cdn.nationalarchives.gov.uk">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cdn.nationalarchives.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Layout 1</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Archives Layout 1</span></span></span>

The second event appears stronger than the first because several officers saw something. Yet the surviving record does not provide six full, separately written accounts with diagrams, angular positions and precise timings. Instead, the principal accessible source is the working party’s collective summary, supplemented by Hubbard’s recollections decades later. It is therefore difficult to determine how closely the witnesses agreed before discussion, whether all perceived a disc, or whether Hubbard’s interpretation influenced the group’s description.

The official report explicitly considered that possibility. Its authors called the incident an example of one report influencing another and argued that Hubbard’s earlier experience may have predisposed the group to interpret a distant aircraft as abnormal. The setting reinforces that possibility: the observers were actively waiting for an experimental aeroplane, and the 1950 Farnborough show involved many prototypes and development aircraft.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cdn.nationalarchives.gov.uk/documents/the-ufo-files-extract.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cdn.nationalarchives.gov.uk">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cdn.nationalarchives.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Layout 1</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Archives Layout 1</span></span></span>

However, the influence argument cannot by itself explain everything. Several trained observers evidently agreed that an object was present and that its motion seemed unusual. Saying they were influenced by expectation describes a possible psychological mechanism; it does not identify the aircraft, establish its distance or reconstruct the reported manoeuvres. The working party’s explanation was plausible but not demonstrated.

## How officials questioned the witnesses

Hubbard reported the first incident through his chain of command. Members of the Ministry of Defence’s Flying Saucer Working Party subsequently questioned him, and the witnesses to the September observation were reportedly interviewed within a day. The group brought together scientific and technical-intelligence representatives from the armed services and Ministry of Defence. Its purpose was not to promote UFO research but to decide whether the reports indicated a threat or intelligence problem during the early Cold War.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cdn.nationalarchives.gov.uk/documents/the-ufo-files-extract.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cdn.nationalarchives.gov.uk">[nationalarchives.gov.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cdn.nationalarchives.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Layout 1</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Archives Layout 1</span></span></span>

Hubbard later recalled being asked about the object’s height, size, speed, identity and possible origin. He said he answered that it had not been designed or built on Earth, an interpretation that went beyond what his observation alone could establish. He also recalled that the September witnesses were warned not to compare accounts or discuss the subject privately. Such separation would have been sensible if intended to prevent contamination, although the surviving public record does not allow the precise interview procedure to be reconstructed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cdn.nationalarchives.gov.uk/documents/the-ufo-files-extract.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cdn.nationalarchives.gov.uk">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cdn.nationalarchives.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Layout 1</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Archives Layout 1</span></span></span>

The working party did not accuse Hubbard of dishonesty. Its report stated that he had honestly described what he believed he saw. Officials nevertheless regarded the first claim as inherently improbable because a large, low-flying and extremely fast unconventional aircraft should have attracted many more observers over a populated, aviation-conscious district. They concluded that he had probably experienced an optical illusion or mistaken an ordinary aircraft’s shape and speed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cdn.nationalarchives.gov.uk/documents/the-ufo-files-extract.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cdn.nationalarchives.gov.uk">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cdn.nationalarchives.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Layout 1</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Archives Layout 1</span></span></span>

For the September event, investigators accepted that some flying object had been observed. They proposed that it was a normal aircraft manoeuvring at extreme visual range, with sunlight, distance and prior expectation creating the apparent disc shape and extraordinary movement. As supporting precedent, the report described an officer who initially saw a circular shining disc through a telescope; when its angle changed and the reflected sunlight disappeared, it became recognisable as an ordinary aircraft.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://cdn.nationalarchives.gov.uk/documents/the-ufo-files-extract.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: cdn.nationalarchives.gov.uk">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">cdn.nationalarchives.gov.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Layout 1</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National Archives Layout 1</span></span></span>

This was a reasonable aviation-based comparison, especially at Farnborough. Sunlight reflected from a distant aircraft can hide wings and fuselage, while banking turns can make a bright spot appear to stop, fall or change direction. Yet the report did not name a specific aircraft responsible for either Farnborough observation, produce flight records matching the reported path or demonstrate the explanation through geometry. Its conclusion rested largely on probability: conventional misidentification was judged much more likely than a radically unfamiliar machine.

## Why the evidence never became decisive

The central weakness is the absence of independent physical or instrumental confirmation. The working party itself acknowledged that UFO reports were almost entirely visual and that no tangible, material or objective evidence had been submitted. It argued that convincing results would require coordinated observers, cameras, radar and sound-location equipment—precisely the forms of corroboration missing from Farnborough.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ufotransparency.com/files/intl-uk-defe-44-119-policy-defe-44-119-dsac-ufo-policy" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufotransparency.com">[UFO Transparency]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufotransparency.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFO TransparencyDEFE 44/119, Defence Scientific Advisory Council UFO Policy, UK Ministry of Defence / Defence Scientific Advisory Council...</span></span></span>

What survives can be separated into three evidential layers:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Contemporary official evidence:** a classified working-party summary confirming that Hubbard and other RAF officers made reports and were interviewed.
* **Later witness testimony:** Hubbard’s much fuller account, recorded in 2002, including detailed descriptions of sound, appearance, smell and reactions.
* **Official interpretation:** the working party’s conclusion that optical effects or conventional aircraft provided the simplest explanation.

</div>

The first layer establishes that the case was real as a reported event and was taken seriously enough to investigate. The second adds valuable human detail but is vulnerable to memory change over five decades. The third supplies plausible explanations, although none was tied conclusively to an identified object.

There are also inconsistencies that weaken precise reconstruction. The official report places the first sighting on 14 August and gives an object about 50 feet across at roughly 5,000 feet; Hubbard’s later version places it on 15 August and estimates 100 feet across at 700–1,000 feet. These are not minor differences when calculating apparent speed or deciding whether other people should have noticed it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ufotransparency.com/files/intl-uk-defe-44-119-policy-defe-44-119-dsac-ufo-policy" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufotransparency.com">[UFO Transparency]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufotransparency.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFO TransparencyDEFE 44/119, Defence Scientific Advisory Council UFO Policy, UK Ministry of Defence / Defence Scientific Advisory Council...</span></span></span>

The September witness count requires similar care. Six officers were present at the initial observation, while Hubbard later remembered the crowd increasing to more than twelve. The official summary discusses the six principal witnesses, but publicly accessible accounts do not reproduce a matching series of individual statements from everyone who joined them. “More than a dozen witnesses” sounds impressive, but the evidential value depends on how many independently observed the full event and recorded their impressions before hearing others’ descriptions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ufotransparency.com/files/intl-uk-defe-44-119-policy-defe-44-119-dsac-ufo-policy" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufotransparency.com">[UFO Transparency]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufotransparency.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFO TransparencyDEFE 44/119, Defence Scientific Advisory Council UFO Policy, UK Ministry of Defence / Defence Scientific Advisory Council...</span></span></span>

## Why the case remains unresolved

Farnborough is unresolved because neither side completed the evidential chain. Hubbard could not establish the object’s distance, dimensions or velocity, and no photograph or instrument record verified its reported characteristics. The working party, meanwhile, offered a general explanation—ordinary aircraft seen under deceptive conditions—without identifying the aircraft or reproducing the observation.

The case should therefore not be described as unexplained proof of an exotic vehicle. “Unresolved” means that the surviving information is insufficient to choose confidently between several possibilities. A distant conventional aircraft, unusual reflection, balloon-like object or combined perceptual effect remains more economical than an unknown advanced craft, but the documentary record is too thin to settle the matter retrospectively.

Later reporting strengthened the case’s historical importance more than its physical claims. The discovery and release of the Flying Saucer Working Party report confirmed that Britain had secretly examined Hubbard’s reports and that officials regarded them as two of the few 1950 incidents worthy of field investigation. Hubbard’s later interview also preserved a detailed first-person narrative that might otherwise have been lost.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.uk-ufo.org/condign/fswpfarn.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: uk-ufo.org">[UK UFO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">uk-ufo.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">endlesham» Secret files Analysis Berwyn Incident» Article Secret Files box The Secret Files» Introdu...</span></span></span>

At the same time, those releases weakened exaggerated versions of the story. They showed that officials did not secretly certify an extraordinary craft. They considered the witnesses sincere but rejected their interpretation, stressing the lack of material evidence and the known difficulty of judging distant objects. The report’s eventual recommendation was that no further investigation should be undertaken unless physical evidence appeared.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://disclosdex.com/programs/1950-flying-saucer-working-party" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: disclosdex.com">[Disclosdex]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">disclosdex.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Flying Saucer Working Party &#124; Disclosdexreports as they appeared, report to DSI/JTIC when necessary, and keep in touch with Ame...</span></span></span>

Farnborough consequently occupies a distinctive place in Hampshire’s UFO history. It is stronger than an anonymous report of a distant light because it involved an experienced pilot, multiple RAF observers and contemporary official inquiry. It is weaker than a scientifically robust case because almost everything important—shape, distance, motion, sound and identity—depends on human perception and memory. Its lasting value lies in that tension: it shows both why qualified witnesses deserve serious attention and why witness credibility alone cannot turn an unidentified sighting into proof of an extraordinary object.
