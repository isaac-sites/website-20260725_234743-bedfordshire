## Introduction

Reports of unknown objects near London Stansted Airport matter primarily because anything sharing the path of a passenger aircraft can become a flight-safety hazard. The strongest [Essex]({{ 'what-really-happened-in-essex-skies/' | relative_url }}) example is a 2022 police log stating that a Ryanair crew, climbing from Stansted at about 4,000 feet, saw a black object pass within an estimated 20 metres. A drone was considered possible, although airport detection equipment reportedly registered nothing. The surviving public record does not establish what the object was, and it is too brief to confirm the distance independently.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.essex.police.uk/foi-ai/essex-police/other-information/previous-foi-requests/ufo-reports-2014-to-2024/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: essex.police.uk">[Essex Police]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">essex.police.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ufo reports 2014 to 2024</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">2022 &#124; HERTFORDSHIRE &#124; RYANAIR WAS CLIMBING OUT FROM STANSTED AIRPORT AND WAS A HEIGHT OF 4000FT AND JUST OVER THE ST ELIZABETH CENTRETHE...</span></span></span>

## What happened in the 2022 Ryanair report?

Essex Police’s released UFO records describe a Ryanair aircraft climbing out of Stansted over the St Elizabeth Centre, near Much Hadham in Hertfordshire. At approximately 4,000 feet, the pilot reportedly saw a black object moving from north to south. The aircraft was said to be travelling at about 230 mph, with the object passing within 20 metres. The entry adds that it was considered a possible drone, but Stansted’s drone-monitoring equipment did not detect it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.essex.police.uk/foi-ai/essex-police/other-information/previous-foi-requests/ufo-reports-2014-to-2024/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: essex.police.uk">[Essex Police]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">essex.police.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ufo reports 2014 to 2024</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">2022 &#124; HERTFORDSHIRE &#124; RYANAIR WAS CLIMBING OUT FROM STANSTED AIRPORT AND WAS A HEIGHT OF 4000FT AND JUST OVER THE ST ELIZABETH CENTRETHE...</span></span></span>

The location is a useful reminder that an incident associated with Stansted need not occur directly over Essex. Aircraft departing the airport quickly cross modern county and police boundaries, and this particular report was logged geographically under Hertfordshire. It nevertheless belongs within Stansted’s Essex aviation history because the flight originated there, the airport’s detection system was consulted and the possible hazard concerned its departure traffic.

The quoted separation sounds alarming, but it should be treated as an estimate rather than a measured miss distance. Pilots can be highly capable observers of nearby traffic, yet judging the range and size of an unfamiliar, briefly seen object is difficult without knowing its dimensions. A small object close to the cockpit and a larger object farther away can occupy a similar apparent size. The police summary supplies no photograph, recovered material, radar track, exact flight details or independent second sighting.

There is also no indication in the published police entry that the event received a completed determination from the UK Airprox Board, the specialist body that assesses reported situations in which aircraft safety may have been compromised. UK Airprox reports normally bring together the pilot’s account, air-traffic information, radar analysis and other available evidence before assigning a risk category. The police log is much thinner: it preserves a potentially serious report, but not a full safety investigation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.airproxboard.org.uk/reports-and-analysis/individual-airprox-reports/2022/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: airproxboard.org.uk">[airproxboard.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">airproxboard.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">individual airprox reports</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">in 2022 &#124; UK Airprox BoardIndividual Airprox reports in 2022 &#124; UK Airprox Board Home Reports and analysis Individual airprox reports Indi...</span></span></span>

The fairest classification is therefore **unresolved but weakly documented**. The report supports the conclusion that a pilot saw something unexpected near the aircraft. It does not demonstrate that the object was an exotic craft, or even prove that it was a drone.

## Why several Stansted aircraft reported an alert

A separate 2022 Stansted entry records three aircraft reporting an object or alert in their vicinity. According to the tower report, two aircraft approaching Runway 22 had indications of something roughly 500 feet below them; the closest aircraft was still about 20 kilometres away. Another aircraft, two or three miles from the airport, received a Traffic Collision Avoidance System alert, but none of the pilots saw an object when they looked.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.essex.police.uk/foi-ai/essex-police/other-information/previous-foi-requests/ufo-reports-2014-to-2024/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: essex.police.uk">[Essex Police]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">essex.police.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ufo reports 2014 to 2024</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">2022 &#124; HERTFORDSHIRE &#124; RYANAIR WAS CLIMBING OUT FROM STANSTED AIRPORT AND WAS A HEIGHT OF 4000FT AND JUST OVER THE ST ELIZABETH CENTRETHE...</span></span></span>

This case is often presented as several pilots independently seeing the same UFO. The police wording does not support that interpretation. It says the crews were alerted to something, while also stating that they could not see it. The difference matters because the evidence came from an electronic warning rather than a common visual observation.

Traffic Collision Avoidance System, usually shortened to TCAS, is designed principally to interrogate and track transponder-equipped aircraft. It can issue a traffic advisory when another responding transponder appears close enough to require attention; a resolution advisory can command a climb or descent when the calculated conflict becomes more urgent. A normal consumer drone, bird, balloon or loose object carries no aviation transponder and would not ordinarily generate such a TCAS track.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://regulatorylibrary.caa.co.uk/air-operations-pre-30-october-2025/Content/Document%20Structure/08%20SPO/4%20GM/GM1%20SPO%20OP%20205%20Airborne%20collision.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: regulatorylibrary.caa.co.uk">[Regulatory Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">regulatorylibrary.caa.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on caa.co.uk.</span></span></span>

The Stansted alert could therefore have involved a conventional aircraft, an incomplete or misleading track, erroneous altitude information, or some other technical or operational issue. The record is not detailed enough to decide between these possibilities. What it does **not** provide is strong evidence that TCAS detected a mysterious physical object invisible to every pilot.

This also shows why “three aircraft reported a UFO” is an oversimplification. Multiple crews reacting to the same traffic information or system target do not necessarily provide three independent confirmations. Their reports may all derive from one underlying electronic indication.

## Drones, birds, balloons and debris

A drone is an obvious concern near Stansted because it can remain airborne, cross an approach path and contain hard components such as batteries, motors and metal fittings. UK airport Flight Restriction Zones prohibit drone or model-aircraft flights without permission. These zones include an area around the aerodrome and extensions aligned with runway approaches, generally reaching 2,000 feet above ground level. Drone operations above 400 feet normally also require specific Civil Aviation Authority authorisation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.caa.co.uk/drones/open-category/moving-on-to-more-advanced-flying/airspace/aerodromes-heliports-and-spaceports/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: caa.co.uk">[CAA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">caa.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on caa.co.uk.</span></span></span>

A suspected drone at 4,000 feet would therefore be well outside ordinary recreational flying rules. That does not make such a height physically impossible, but it raises other possibilities. Pilots across Britain have reported presumed drones at unexpectedly high altitudes, yet UK Airprox assessments sometimes retain the label “unknown object” when the description is insufficient to identify a drone confidently. Radar searches commonly find no associated contact because small objects may be difficult to detect and ordinary drones do not respond to secondary surveillance radar.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.airproxboard.org.uk/uploadedFiles/Content/Standard_content/Airprox_report_files/2022/Airprox%20Report%202022031.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: airproxboard.org.uk">[airproxboard.org.uk]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">airproxboard.org.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Consolidated Drone/Balloon/Model/Unknown Object</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Consolidated Drone/Balloon/Model/Unknown Object</span></span></span>

Failure of an airport drone detector to register an object is not proof that no drone was present. Counter-drone systems may combine radio-frequency detection, radar, optical cameras, thermal imaging and acoustic sensors, each with different blind spots. A drone flying autonomously may emit little useful control traffic; its body may be too small or poorly positioned for radar or cameras; and weather, terrain or surrounding radio activity may reduce performance. Research into multi-sensor detection therefore emphasises combining several methods rather than treating one negative result as conclusive.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.01927" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on arxiv.org.</span></span></span>

Birds remain a serious alternative. A dark bird crossing close to a fast-moving cockpit can appear only briefly, leaving little time to identify wings or movement. Wildlife strikes are recognised by the Civil Aviation Authority as a significant aviation threat and are subject to mandatory occurrence reporting. The fact that birds are familiar does not make them harmless, particularly during take-off and landing when aircraft are low and crews have limited room to manoeuvre.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.caa.co.uk/birdstrikereporting" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: caa.co.uk">[CAA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">caa.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on caa.co.uk.</span></span></span>

Balloons and airborne debris are also plausible in some reports. Foil balloons can look metallic or black depending on lighting and viewing angle, while plastic sheeting, advertising material and other windborne objects may tumble or appear to move purposefully during a short encounter. Such [explanations]({{ 'explanations/' | relative_url }}) become harder or easier depending on the weather, altitude, shape and direction of travel, but those details are absent from the Ryanair police summary.

The important point is that “unidentified” describes the state of the evidence, not a special class of aircraft. Several ordinary candidates remain possible, and each carries a different safety profile.

## What pilots and sensors can establish

A pilot report is often the first and sometimes the only warning that an unexpected object has entered controlled airspace. Crews can provide time, position, altitude, colour, apparent direction and estimated separation. Immediate reporting also allows air-traffic controllers to warn following aircraft, inform airport authorities and preserve radar or communications data before it is overwritten.

Visual testimony is strongest when the object is seen for more than a moment, by more than one crew member, from several aircraft or ground positions, and when descriptions agree independently. It becomes weaker when the encounter lasts a fraction of a second, the object’s size is unknown or the estimated range is reconstructed afterwards. A pilot’s professional status deserves weight, but it does not remove the optical difficulties involved.

Conventional air-traffic radar can help identify aircraft with working transponders and may sometimes record larger non-cooperative targets on primary radar. It is not a complete map of every physical object in the sky. TCAS is narrower still: its collision-avoidance functions depend on replies from aircraft transponders, so the absence of a TCAS alert says little about a bird or ordinary drone, while the presence of an alert usually points towards a transponder-related target rather than an inert object.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://regulatorylibrary.caa.co.uk/air-operations-pre-30-october-2025/Content/Document%20Structure/06%20NCC/4%20GM/GM1%20NCC%20OP%20220%20Airborne%20collision.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: regulatorylibrary.caa.co.uk">[Regulatory Library]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">regulatorylibrary.caa.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on caa.co.uk.</span></span></span>

Dedicated drone detection adds another layer but is not infallible. A positive detection may establish location, movement and possibly the type or radio signature of a drone. A negative result merely means that the system did not identify a target under those particular conditions. Investigators still need to compare the pilot’s account with radar data, authorised drone activity, weather, bird movements, balloons, other aircraft and any available video.

The most valuable evidence would therefore include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* precise cockpit and air-traffic-control timings;
* independent descriptions from both pilots;
* primary and secondary radar recordings;
* the exact nature of any TCAS advisory;
* logs from Stansted’s drone-detection equipment;
* weather and wind information;
* reports from following aircraft or witnesses on the ground;
* inspection of the aircraft for impact marks or biological material.

</div>

None of that supporting material appears in the public Essex Police summary of the Ryanair encounter. Its absence does not disprove the event, but it sharply limits what can responsibly be claimed.

## Could an unknown object endanger a Stansted flight?

Yes. An unidentified object does not need to be technologically mysterious to endanger an aircraft. A collision with a drone, bird, balloon payload or dense debris could damage a windscreen, engine, leading edge or other exposed structure. An evasive manoeuvre can also create risk, especially during a busy departure or approach when crews are managing speed, configuration, terrain and other traffic.

Stansted’s operating environment makes timely classification particularly important. The airport handles commercial passenger traffic, cargo aircraft and business flights, with repeated arrivals and departures channelled along predictable routes. A single credible report may therefore affect several following aircraft even when the object is never found. Controllers may pass warnings, alter routing or increase surveillance while police and airport personnel assess whether the event suggests illegal drone activity.

At the same time, flight safety should not be confused with proof of extraordinary origin. The 2022 Ryanair case contains a credible safety concern but little publicly available investigative detail. The separate multi-aircraft incident appears to have centred on a TCAS indication without visual confirmation, making conventional traffic or a technical explanation more plausible than a hidden craft detected by the aircraft.

Within Essex UFO history, Stansted’s reports are significant because they show how the meaning of “UFO” has changed. In a police database it may simply be an administrative label for something not immediately identified. For pilots and controllers, the first question is not whether it came from elsewhere, but whether it occupies airspace that must remain safe. On the evidence currently public, the Stansted cases justify careful reporting and investigation, but not claims that alien or otherwise extraordinary vehicles have been established near the airport.

    closeMenu(section);
  }
  sections.forEach(function (section) {
    if (section.getAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init') === '1') return;
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init', '1');
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    var marketId = defaultMarket;
    if (window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation && window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket) {
      marketId = window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket(available, {
        storageKey: 'phoenix-ebay-market',
        defaultMarket: defaultMarket
      });
    } else if (available.indexOf(defaultMarket)< 0) {
      marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    }
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      select.addEventListener('change', function () {
        applyMarket(section, select.value, true);
      });
    }
    var trigger = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-trigger]');
    var menu = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-menu]');
    if (trigger && menu) {
      trigger.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
        event.stopPropagation();
        var expanded = trigger.getAttribute('aria-expanded') === 'true';
        menu.hidden = expanded;
        trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', expanded ? 'false' : 'true');
        var picker = trigger.closest('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
        if (picker) picker.classList.toggle('fr-ebay-market-picker--open', !expanded);
      });
      Array.prototype.slice.call(menu.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
        button.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
          event.stopPropagation();
          applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option'), true);
        });
      });
      document.addEventListener('click', function () { closeMenu(section); });
    }
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.addEventListener('click', function () {
        applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'), true);
      });
    });
    applyMarket(section, marketId, false);
  });
})();
